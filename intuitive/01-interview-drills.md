# Intuitive Surgical — Interview Drills

### Fifteen questions · Senior Human Factors Analyst · Sunnyvale

Companion to the study guide. The guide is what you read in the car; **this is what you rehearse out loud.**

Fifteen questions, each with the question as it will actually be asked, one model answer, three scoped follow-ups, and the line that moves you from Senior to Staff-signal.

> **One answer per question — however they word it.** Interviewers rephrase. The claim, the evidence, and the boundary stay identical across every room, because they compare notes afterward.

Anything in `[brackets]` is a fact only you can supply. **Fill it in or delete the sentence.** Never improvise a number.

---

## Two structures. Using the wrong one costs you even when the content is right.

Every question below is tagged **A** or **B**. Tell a story in answer to a method question and it reads as dodging; recite method in answer to an experience question and it reads as evasive.

### Structure A — experience, behavioral, cross-functional

| Beat | Words | What it must do |
|---|---|---|
| Situation | 20–30 | One sentence. Where, the stakes, why it was hard. |
| Task | 15–25 | Your specific charge, in first person. |
| Action | 90–120 | "I," not "we." The hardest moment is the centerpiece. Name what you decided against. |
| Result | 25–40 | Quantified where the evidence supports it. Attribution honest. |
| Earned secret | 20–30 | The thing you know that most people in your role don't. |
| Mechanism | 20–30 | The standard, gate, or artifact you left behind. |

**200–265 words ≈ 90–110 seconds.**

The mechanism beat is the gate between Senior and Staff. Without it you've described excellent execution, which is the baseline. Say it as its own sentence: *"What I left behind was X, and Y still has to clear it."*

### Structure B — methodological, technical, regulatory

| Beat | Words | What it must do |
|---|---|---|
| The claim | 20–30 | Lead with the position. No windup. |
| How I'd know | 40–60 | The method, or how the effect actually works. |
| Evidence, and where it stops | 40–60 | Your evidence, then its boundary — before anyone asks. |
| The decision it drives | 30–45 | The design input, threshold, or gate this produces. |
| What would change my mind | 20–35 | The result that would overturn the claim. |

**160–230 words ≈ 65–90 seconds.** Follow-ups: 45–90 words ≈ 20–35 seconds.

Naming the limit of your own evidence, unprompted, buys more credibility than any extra proof point.

### The register check

*"We ran a study and reported findings"* is baseline. *"I set the perceptual threshold that latency budgets had to clear"* is the level you're interviewing at. Hedges — *"I sort of drove," "they let me," "I was involved in"* — cap you regardless of how good the work was. Delete them for the day.

## EQ01 — The opener

**Asked by:** everyone, first · **Target:** ~75s · **Structure:** special — not STAR

> **Base:** "Tell me about yourself." / "Walk me through your background." / "What brings you here?"

This one isn't a story. It's three moves: what you do, why here, and the arc — delivered before they can ask about the arc.

### Model answer (~185 words / 75s)

**What I do.** "I turn human perceptual and cognitive limits into design inputs that engineers can verify — in systems where a use error causes physical harm.

**Why here, three reasons.** First, I want the consequence back. I do my best work when a use error means physical harm, not a bad quarter. Second, the problem is multimodal — visual, haptic, and auditory interaction under load is what I've spent my career measuring. And third, it's a return to where I started: my graduate research was on interruptions during surgical and clinical tasks. This is the first role I've seen that uses all three.

**The arc.** Depth, then building, and now commitment. Four and a half years at Amazon going deep on a multi-year research program. Then EchoStar, where I lead human factors strategy across the organization. What I want now is a regulated domain worth staying in for a long time. That's a deliberate choice, not a reaction to anything."

### Follow-ups

**F1 — "Why leave EchoStar?"** *(~55 words)*

> "Nothing's pushing me out — I'm leading the function and the work is good. What EchoStar can't give me is consequence. I want to be somewhere a use error means physical harm, in a domain where the work compounds over a long horizon. That's a narrow set of places, and this is near the top of it."

**F2 — "You're at Staff now. This role is Senior."** *(~40 words)*

> "Scope and domain matter more to me than the title on the offer. The work described in this role is the work I want to be doing, and I'd rather be doing it here at Senior than something adjacent somewhere else."

**F3 — "This is fully onsite in Sunnyvale. Where are you?"** *(~35 words)*

> "I'm planning on the South Bay — `[Sunnyvale / specific area]` — and I'd be there within `[X weeks]` of an offer. `[One concrete step already taken.]` There's nothing on my end holding it up."

> ⚠️ **Four beats: place, timeframe, evidence, closure.** Under fifteen seconds, no hedging. The evidence beat is the one that does the work. And **compensation has not been discussed with Intuitive at all** — if it comes up, *"I'd like to understand the level and scope first."*

### Senior → Staff-signal

- **Senior:** gives a clear, confident summary of their background.
- **Staff-signal:** gets to the arc question before the interviewer does, and frames the moves as a thesis rather than a sequence.
- **Say this:** *"What I want now is a regulated domain worth staying in for a long time."*

## EQ02 — The research you're proudest of ★ your opener story

**Asked by:** HF peers, clinical engineers · **Target:** ~90s · **Structure:** A

> **Base:** "Tell me about the research you're most proud of." / "Walk me through your graduate work." / "What's the deepest research you've done?"

> ⚠️ **Fix this before you speak to anyone.** The **laparoscopy study is your master's thesis (2017)**. Your **dissertation is the data-entry study (2020)**. Your résumé has them backwards. Both are public under your name in ODU's repository, and these people have PhDs.

### Model answer (~230 words / 92s)

**Situation.** "I spent five years at Old Dominion on a single question: what an interruption actually costs someone doing medical work.

**Task.** My master's thesis put that question inside a laparoscopic skills trainer, and it became an HFES paper. But the manipulation had a problem I'll come back to, so my dissertation moved it into a controlled medical data-entry task where I could isolate what actually drives the cost.

**Action.** Thirty-six participants, twenty-seven interruptions each, every one exactly eighteen seconds so duration couldn't confound anything. I crossed two factors: how hard the interruption was — blank, one-back, two-back — against whether the reminder afterward pointed at the exact field they'd left, something related, or something unrelated. And the methodological move I care most about: when the interruption ended, I wiped everything they'd entered and reset the cursor to the top-left. The two closest studies in the literature left the cursor in place or the instructions on screen, so their participants could have been reading the answer off the display rather than remembering it. I removed every visual and spatial cue, so the only thing left was memory — which is what the measure was supposed to be about.

**Result.** Difficulty dominated. Once an interruption demanded working memory, resumption errors roughly tripled. And a reminder only helped when it pointed at the exact place they'd left.

**Earned secret.** Thirty of my thirty-six participants were rehearsing — holding their place in their head — and rehearsal failed them precisely when the interruption got hard. The user's own coping strategy collapses exactly when they need it most.

**Mechanism.** So the principle I still design against: getting the user back to where they were is the system's job, not the user's."

> ⚠️ **Verify every figure against the dissertation PDF before you say it out loud.** These came from a summary, not the document.

### Follow-ups

**F1 — "What were you actually testing between, theoretically?"** *(~55 words)*

> "The three theories were `[name 1]`, `[name 2]`, and `[name 3]`. The data separated them because `[one sentence — which prediction held, which failed]`."

> ⚠️ **Fill this in before you use the word "theories" anywhere.** If you can't name all three and say in one sentence how the data told them apart, cut the reference and describe the findings on their own. A half-remembered theory name is worse than none.

**F2 — "Your participants were undergraduates. Why should that transfer to surgeons?"** *(~75 words)*

> "It transfers as a mechanism, not as a number. Resumption cost is a working-memory property, and working memory doesn't care about your credential. What changes in an operating room is how often interruptions happen, what they cost when they do, and whether a team absorbs them. So I'd carry it forward as a hypothesis with a formative study attached — never as a threshold. And you're right to press: those were novices by design."

**F3 — "What does that mean for a console surgeon?"** *(~70 words)*

> "The console surgeon is visually isolated from the room, so essentially every communication from the bedside arrives as an interruption rather than something they can absorb peripherally. `[author, year]` found an interruption roughly every six minutes in general surgery. If that holds in a robotic case, the design question is what the system owes someone on re-entry — not whether we can train people to interrupt less."

> ⚠️ **Fill in the citations or soften to "one study found."** Several people in this loop will know this literature. Being caught on a borrowed number costs more than the number ever bought.

### Senior → Staff-signal

- **Senior:** describes the study and reports the findings accurately.
- **Staff-signal:** names the confound they engineered out, explains why it mattered relative to the prior literature, and converts the finding into a design principle.
- **Say this:** *"Getting the user back to where they were is the system's job, not the user's."*

## EQ03 — The study that went wrong ★ don't waste this on a small failure

**Asked by:** HF peers, senior HF · **Target:** ~95s · **Structure:** A

> **Base:** "Tell me about a study that didn't go the way you planned." / "Tell me about a failure." / "When has your research been wrong?"

> ⚠️ **Reread the HFES 2018 paper first.** It's published, findable, and co-authored. Make sure this telling matches how the paper framed the results — it's the one document an interviewer can check against you in real time.

### Model answer (~250 words / 100s)

**Situation.** "My master's thesis was supposed to measure how long it takes to get back to a laparoscopic task after an interruption.

**Task.** The setup was the standard laparoscopic skills exercise — moving six colored rings in a memorized order through a trainer box. And I interrupted people the way the literature says surgeons actually get interrupted: somebody talks to you about something unrelated. `[author, year]` found that accounts for about a quarter of all interruptions in an operating room.

**Action.** Forty-six of my fifty-one participants never stopped. They just kept moving rings while they answered me. So they were *distracted*, not interrupted — and the dependent variable I'd built the whole study around stopped existing. A six-person pilot had shown me the opposite, which is exactly why I'd trusted it. I couldn't rescue the primary measure, so I reported the null honestly and analyzed what the data could actually support, which turned out to be a clean task-demand effect on sequence errors.

**Result.** What I actually learned is that interruption and distraction are different phenomena, and which one you get isn't a property of the stimulus — it's a property of the person and the moment. I'd designed it as though it were a property of the stimulus. That was my error, not the participants'.

**Earned secret.** Six people told me my manipulation worked. Fifty-one told me it didn't. A pilot that confirms you is the most dangerous result you can get.

**Mechanism.** So I wrote the fix into the document — use a visual blackout instead of a conversation, which forces a genuine suspension and gives you an exact interval. That's the design the dissertation used. And it's why I now pilot the manipulation check separately from the effect, and why I get stakeholders to agree up front on what each result would change."

### Follow-ups

**F1 — "What would you have done differently at design time?"** *(~60 words)*

> "Piloted the manipulation check as its own study rather than folding it into a feasibility run. I was piloting whether the task worked and whether the interruption worked at the same time, with six people, and the task signal masked the interruption failure. Those are two different questions and they needed two different pilots."

**F2 — "How did you handle reporting a null to your committee?"** *(~55 words)*

> "Directly, and early. I brought the manipulation-check data before I brought the analysis, so the conversation was about what the study could still answer rather than about whether I'd failed. The paper reports the null explicitly. I'd rather publish a manipulation that didn't work than quietly reframe the hypothesis around whatever turned out significant."

**F3 — "How does that apply to validation testing here?"** *(~70 words)*

> "The same failure mode is live in any simulated-use study: you assume the scenario induced the condition you designed it to induce. If your use scenario is supposed to create time pressure and participants don't actually feel it, your use errors aren't telling you what you think. So I'd treat the scenario itself as something to validate — with a check that doesn't depend on asking people whether they felt rushed."

### Senior → Staff-signal

- **Senior:** tells a real failure honestly and says what they learned.
- **Staff-signal:** identifies the *class* of error, and leaves behind a practice that prevents it recurring — for other people, not just themselves.
- **Say this:** *"A pilot that confirms you is the most dangerous result you can get."*

## EQ04 — The unarticulated need ★ rehearse this one hardest

**Asked by:** hiring manager, senior HF, designers · **Target:** ~95s · **Structure:** A

> **Base:** "Tell me about a time you identified a user need nobody had articulated." / "How have you worked in ambiguity?" / "Tell me about 0→1 research."

**This is the center of gravity for the role.** The job description is almost entirely formative work, defining needs, and authoring design inputs. If one answer decides this loop, it's this one.

> ⚠️ **The specifics below are slots, and they're deliberately empty.** These are exactly the details a panel probes and compares notes on. The shape is ready; the facts have to be yours.

### Model answer (~240 words / 95s)

**Situation.** "Echo Hub was a genuinely new kind of device — a control panel mounted on a wall and shared by a whole household. There was no prior product to test against, so there was no usage data and nothing to run a study on.

**Task.** My charge was to define what the thing needed to do for people before there was a design to evaluate. The first question was `[the user need nobody had defined — for example, what someone needs from it in a two-second glance while walking past]`.

**Action.** I ran `[named generative method — in-home contextual inquiry, a diary study, observation of how households control devices today]` with `[who, and how many]`. What came out was `[the non-obvious need]`. The reason nobody had written it down is that `[why it was invisible — the workaround looked like ordinary behavior]`. A single qualitative source isn't enough to move an architecture decision, so I checked it against `[second, independent source]`, which pointed the same way. Then I translated it into something an engineer could actually verify rather than a recommendation they could interpret: the device shall `[verifiable requirement — glance legibility at X distance / primary controls reachable without navigation / reach envelope for Y percent of adults at mount height]`.

**Result.** That shaped `[portfolio-level decision — layout priority, mounting guidance, default screen behavior]`.

**Earned secret.** The needs that don't get written down are the ones where the workaround looks like ordinary behavior. Nobody reports it as a problem, because from the inside it isn't one — it's just what you do.

**Mechanism.** What I left behind was `[the requirement, guideline, or framework]`, and `[team]` still designs against it."

### Follow-ups

**F1 — "How did you know that was a real need and not just something a few people said?"** *(~70 words)*

> "Two things. One, I don't move an architecture decision on a single qualitative source, so I looked for it in `[second, independent source]` — different method, same conclusion. Two, I was looking for a compensating behavior rather than a stated preference. What people say they want is unreliable; what they've already built a workaround for is evidence, because they paid a cost for it without being asked."

**F2 — "Why should that transfer here? It's a consumer product."** *(~70 words)*

> "The limit is real: it's a household, and there's no clinical consequence when you get it wrong. The method transfers, the stakes don't. But structurally it's the same problem as a new console capability — no fielded product to test against, several roles sharing one system, and each of them with seconds of attention to spare. You have to find the need in the environment before you have anything to evaluate."

**F3 — "What were the adoption numbers?"** *(~45 words)*

> "I'd be careful with specifics there, but at the portfolio level it shaped `[portfolio-level decision]`. I'd rather give you the reasoning and the requirement than a figure I shouldn't be repeating."

> ⚠️ **Portfolio level only.** No Echo Hub or Echo Show number — adoption, engagement, sales, any of it. Refusing well reads as discipline to a medical device company, and it's the same instinct they want you to have about *their* data.

### Senior → Staff-signal

- **Senior:** finds an insight and reports it persuasively.
- **Staff-signal:** explains why the need was structurally invisible, corroborates it across sources, and converts it into a *shall* statement someone else can verify without them.
- **Say this:** *"The needs that don't get written down are the ones where the workaround looks like ordinary behavior."*

## EQ05 — Pushing back

**Asked by:** senior HF, engineers, PMs · **Target:** ~95s · **Structure:** A

> **Base:** "Tell me about a time you disagreed with engineering." / "How do you handle it when your recommendation is rejected?" / The job description says *negotiate design recommendations* — expect this.

> ⚠️ **This is a shape, not a script.** Fill it with a real disagreement. The four beats are what make it land; the content has to be yours.

### Model answer (~235 words / 95s)

**Situation.** "`[The program, and the recommendation you made.]` It carried a real cost — `[schedule / money / performance]` — and `[who]` pushed back.

**Task.** My job was to either move the decision or accept it honestly, not to be right in a meeting.

**Action.** I started by granting the objection out loud, because it was a legitimate one: `[their objection, stated fairly]`. A researcher who thinks engineering is simply wrong reads as junior, and you lose the room before you've started. Then I argued from the strongest ground I had, in order. First, the risk analysis and the severity of the potential harm — not how many users hit it, but what happens to the one who does. Second, converging evidence: `[two independent sources]` pointed the same way, which is harder to dismiss than one study. Third — and this is the part that actually moved it — I built them a cheaper alternative. `[The alternative you scoped for them.]` I didn't escalate. Escalation is available, but it spends credit you need later and it makes the next disagreement harder.

**Result.** `[What was adopted, and what you conceded — there's always something.]` If I'd conceded nothing, I wasn't negotiating, I was waiting.

**Earned secret.** I don't win these by being right. I win them by making the residual risk visible enough that accepting it requires a signature. Once a decision has a name attached, the conversation changes character entirely.

**Mechanism.** What I left behind was `[the gate, criterion, or review step]`, so the argument didn't have to happen again on the next program."

### Follow-ups

**F1 — "What did you concede?"** *(~50 words)*

> "`[The concession.]` I gave ground on `[scope / timing / the less severe branch]` because the evidence there was genuinely thinner, and holding every position equally hard is how you get treated as someone to route around rather than consult."

**F2 — "What if they'd said no anyway?"** *(~60 words)*

> "Then it becomes a documented residual risk with an owner's name on it, and I move on without relitigating it. What I don't do is quietly agree, or keep raising it in side channels. The record is the mechanism — if the risk materializes later, the analysis shows it was identified, quantified, and accepted deliberately."

**F3 — "How do you keep that from making you the team's obstacle?"** *(~65 words)*

> "By showing up early enough that I'm shaping the design rather than grading it. Most of the friction in this job comes from human factors arriving after the architecture is fixed, when every finding is expensive. If I'm in at concept, my constraint is one input among several instead of a late veto — and I'm arguing with a whiteboard, not a schedule."

### Senior → Staff-signal

- **Senior:** advocates effectively and makes the case with evidence.
- **Staff-signal:** grants the counter-argument, names the concession, and converts a won argument into a standing gate.
- **Say this:** *"I don't win these by being right. I win them by making the residual risk visible enough that accepting it requires a signature."*

## EQ06 — Getting research acted on ★ your signature habit

**Asked by:** PMs, senior HF, hiring manager · **Target:** ~85s · **Structure:** A

> **Base:** "How do you make sure your research actually gets used?" / "Tell me about influencing without authority." / "What do you do when findings get ignored?"

### Model answer (~215 words / 86s)

**Situation.** "Early in my career I watched good studies land in a findings meeting and change nothing, and I stopped believing the problem was the quality of the research or how well I presented it.

**Task.** So I changed what I do before a study runs rather than after.

**Action.** Before a study runs, I get the stakeholders to agree in writing on what each possible result will change. Not "we'll consider the findings" — the actual branches. On the latency program that looked like this: if the measured threshold came in `[tighter]` than the current target, the target changes. If it came in `[looser]`, we stop spending engineering cycles on latency and move that effort to `[X]`. Both branches, written down, before anyone had a result to defend. It came in `[result]`, and `[what changed]` — without anyone relitigating whether the research counted, because that argument had already happened `[months]` earlier.

**Result.** The findings meeting stops being a persuasion exercise and becomes the execution of a decision somebody already owns.

**Earned secret.** Findings don't get ignored because they're weak. They get ignored because nobody ever owned the decision they were supposed to inform. Pre-commitment moves the argument to a moment when nobody's ego is attached to an outcome yet.

**Mechanism.** And it cuts both ways — it kills my own studies. If no plausible result changes a decision, I don't run it: `[one example of a study you actually cancelled]`."

> ⚠️ **Name both branches, and make the cancelled study real.** Both branches are what prove it was a genuine pre-commitment and not a prediction you got right. The cancelled study is what makes this read as judgment rather than process — and it only works if it's concrete.

### Follow-ups

**F1 — "What if a stakeholder won't pre-commit?"** *(~65 words)*

> "That's useful information, and I treat it as a scoping finding rather than an obstacle. Usually it means one of two things: the decision isn't actually theirs, so I'm talking to the wrong person, or there's no real decision pending and the study is reassurance. Either way I'd rather find that out before I spend six weeks than after."

**F2 — "How does that work against a development gate?"** *(~65 words)*

> "It maps cleanly. The formative plan says: if people fail this task at this rate, the design changes — and here's the alternative we've already scoped. That turns human factors from a reporting function into something that holds a gate, and it makes the research schedule predictable, because the decision points are named before the work starts."

**F3 — "Isn't that just pre-registration?"** *(~55 words)*

> "It borrows the logic, but pre-registration commits *me* to an analysis. This commits the *stakeholder* to a decision. Those are different problems — one protects against my bias, the other protects against the finding being politely received and shelved. I do both, but the second one is what changes whether the work matters."

### Senior → Staff-signal

- **Senior:** communicates findings clearly and builds relationships to drive adoption.
- **Staff-signal:** restructures *when* the decision gets made, and applies the same discipline to killing their own work.
- **Say this:** *"If no plausible result changes a decision, I don't run the study."*

## EQ07 — Safety-critical work

**Asked by:** clinical engineers, HF peers, regulatory · **Target:** ~85s · **Structure:** A

> **Base:** "Tell me about work where the consequences were serious." / "What's your experience in safety-critical domains?" / "Have you done use-error analysis?"

### Model answer (~215 words / 86s)

**Situation.** "At NASA Langley I worked on the medical workstation for the Lunar Gateway — the place where a crew member would actually be treated, with no evacuation option and no second clinician.

**Task.** I want to be accurate about attribution: I was a PhD intern contributor on that team, not the lead. My piece was the use-error analysis and the simulated-use testing against the candidate workspace layouts.

**Action.** I worked through the task set to find the places where a mistake leads to harm rather than to inconvenience, then traced how those errors actually happen — what the person sees, what they're holding, what they have to remember, what the layout makes easy. Then I redesigned the layout and the information displays off the back of that analysis. The important decision was that I changed the physical arrangement instead of adding a warning, even though the warning was faster and cheaper.

**Result.** Task completion time came down about thirty percent, and critical input errors went to zero.

**Earned secret.** What I was really doing was a use-related risk analysis before I had that vocabulary for it. The reasoning was identical — identify the tasks where an error causes harm, trace the sequence, move the fix into the design.

**Mechanism.** And that ordering isn't a preference, it's the risk control hierarchy: design it out first, protect against it second, and tell people about it last — because a label is the weakest control there is."

### Follow-ups

**F1 — "How did you identify which tasks were critical?"** *(~70 words)*

> "By the severity of the harm if the task goes wrong, not by how often it goes wrong. That's the part people get backwards. You can't estimate the probability of a use error reliably — that's precisely why the framework prioritizes on severity. So the question is never 'how likely is this,' it's 'what happens to the patient if it does,' and anything with an irreversible outcome goes on the list."

**F2 — "What would you have done with more time?"** *(~60 words)*

> "Tested the redesign against a fatigued, task-loaded operator rather than a rested one. The layouts were evaluated under reasonable conditions, and the realistic condition for that workstation is someone who's been awake too long and is frightened. That's the gap I'd close, and I'd expect some of the error reduction to shrink under it."

**F3 — "Zero critical errors sounds like a small sample."** *(~60 words)*

> "It was, and I wouldn't present that as a validation result — it's a formative finding on a limited sample. What it justifies is a design decision, not a safety claim. The honest statement is that the redesign eliminated a specific error mode we'd observed repeatedly, not that the interface is error-free."

### Senior → Staff-signal

- **Senior:** describes safety-critical work accurately with honest attribution.
- **Staff-signal:** names the risk control hierarchy as the reason for the design choice, and volunteers the limit of a clean result before being pushed.
- **Say this:** *"I changed the layout rather than adding a warning. A label is the weakest control there is."*

## EQ08 — Working with clinicians

**Asked by:** clinical engineers, hiring manager · **Target:** ~80s · **Structure:** A

> **Base:** "What's your experience working with surgeons?" / "How do you build credibility with clinical experts?" / "You'd be representing us with surgeons internationally."

**They're screening for clinical humility.** The failure mode here is overclaiming, and it's fatal with this audience.

### Model answer (~205 words / 82s)

**Situation.** "My clinical exposure comes from Brigham and Women's, at Harvard Medical School, where I worked on imaging and physiological stress research.

**Task.** I was running clinical research protocols in a hospital environment — IRB and HIPAA compliance, longitudinal patient data, and coordinating around clinical staff whose actual job was patients, not my study.

**Action.** What that taught me is mostly about how to behave. You are a guest, your study is the least important thing happening in the room, and the fastest way to lose access is to cost a clinician time they didn't agree to give. So I over-prepare: I learn the procedure before I show up, so I'm asking about someone's reasoning rather than making them narrate steps I could have read.

**Result.** I can operate in a clinical environment without being a liability, and my international field research — Uber in Latin America — covers working across language and cultural context.

**Earned secret.** My job in an operating room is to be the least disruptive person in it and still leave with the observation nobody wrote down.

**Mechanism.** And the way I earn a surgeon's time is by showing up having already studied the procedure."

> ⚠️ **Both your thesis and dissertation used undergraduate participants, not clinicians.** If it comes up, say so plainly and immediately — *"Those were controlled studies with novice participants by design."* Never let an interviewer discover it.

### Follow-ups

**F1 — "Have you run studies with surgeons as participants?"** *(~50 words)*

> "Not as participants — my graduate studies used novices by design, because I was isolating a cognitive mechanism rather than measuring expert performance. My clinical work has been research protocols alongside clinical staff at Brigham, and observational rather than experimental. That's a real gap and it's one of the things that draws me here."

**F2 — "A surgeon tells you your finding is wrong. What do you do?"** *(~70 words)*

> "Take it seriously, because on their procedure they're usually right and I'm usually missing context. Then separate the two things: are they disagreeing with the observation, or with my interpretation of it? Those have different fixes. If it's the observation, I go back and look again. If it's the interpretation, that's a genuinely useful conversation and it's why I wanted them in the room."

**F3 — "How would you get surgeon time for generative work?"** *(~60 words)*

> "Make it cheap for them and valuable to them. Short sessions anchored to a specific remembered case rather than open-ended interviews, scheduled around their day instead of mine, and I'd come back with what changed because of what they said. The second session is much easier to get if the first one visibly went somewhere."

### Senior → Staff-signal

- **Senior:** demonstrates comfort in clinical settings and appropriate humility.
- **Staff-signal:** volunteers the gap in their own clinical sampling before being asked, and describes a repeatable way of earning expert access.
- **Say this:** *"My job in an operating room is to be the least disruptive person in it and still leave with the observation nobody wrote down."*

## MQ01 — The haptics question ★ expect a version of this

**Asked by:** hiring manager, senior HF, engineers · **Target:** ~3 min · **Structure:** B, extended

> **Base:** "How would you approach research for force feedback on a surgical system?" / any "how would you research X" hypothetical.

**Announce the sequence before you use it**, so they can follow you: *"Let me take that in order. What already exists, what's already known, what I'd ask experts, what I'd test once there's something to test, and what it all has to turn into."*

### Model answer (~380 words / 3 min — this one is deliberately long)

**The claim.** "The first question isn't whether surgeons want force feedback. It's what their current visual compensation strategy is doing for them, and what restoring force changes about it.

**What already exists.** Three reference points bound this problem. Open surgery, where the surgeon feels tissue directly and their whole model is built on that. Laparoscopic, where force arrives through a long instrument and a pivot — attenuated and distorted, and surgeons have been adapting to that degraded signal for decades. And current robotic practice, where surgeons compensate visually: they read how tissue deforms instead of feeling resistance.

**What's already known.** Before I generate anything, I'd go through the complaint data and the known use problems on the fielded system, plus the literature. Known use problems are free evidence and the cheapest hypotheses I'll ever get — and they're a required input to the process anyway.

**What I'd ask experts.** Cognitive task analysis with expert surgeons about how they work *now* — not 'would you like force feedback,' but: what cues are you actually using, when would force information have changed a decision, where do you currently feel blind. I'd anchor those in specific remembered cases rather than generalities, because that's where the real reasoning surfaces. Contextual inquiry in the OR. And I'd map the workflow across the whole team — the bedside assistant and the scrub are users too.

**What I'd test.** Once there's something to react to, cognitive walkthroughs, then formative testing at increasing fidelity, each round scoped to a decision agreed in advance.

**The decision it drives.** The output isn't a report. It's a set of verifiable design inputs — a detectable force difference, a latency bound, a defined behavior when feedback drops out — plus the first entries in the use-related risk analysis. And those early entries are what the submission gets argued from, years later.

**What would change my mind.** If the cognitive task analysis shows the visual compensation strategy is *better* than force in the cases that matter — more accurate, less effortful — then force feedback is solving a problem surgeons already solved, and the work shifts to not disrupting it."

> ⚠️ **One precision point. Don't blur this.** **Cognitive task analysis** studies how experts think in current practice, needs no prototype, and **generates**. **Cognitive walkthroughs and formative testing** evaluate a design, need something to look at, and **evaluate**. Using them interchangeably tells a specialist you learned the vocabulary rather than the practice.

### Follow-ups

**F1 — "What are the risks of adding force feedback?"** *(~90 words)*

> "Six, and the one people miss is negative transfer. Experienced robotic surgeons have *trained* the visual compensation strategy — new force cues may interfere with a skill they've built. I'd measure that as an expert-versus-novice performance gap, and I'd expect experts to do worse initially. The others: visual and haptic disagreeing, which is worse than one cue alone; latency, where force arriving late stops feeling like it came from the instrument; over-trust, where visual cross-checking drops off; habituation over a long case; and losing feedback mid-case, which is an unannounced mode change."

**F2 — "Why not just put a force number on the screen?"** *(~85 words)*

> "They have different workload profiles, not different quality levels. A visual indicator is cheaper, easier to verify, and degrades gracefully — but it spends the visual channel, which is already the bottleneck in the operative field, and it costs an attention shift. True haptics uses an unloaded channel and needs no attention shift, but brings latency, calibration, and the risk of disagreeing with the eyes. Which is right depends on where the surgeon's spare capacity actually is during that specific critical task — and that's empirical, before the architecture gets chosen."

**F3 — "You have six weeks and no prototype. What do you do?"** *(~75 words)*

> "Everything upstream of the prototype, which is most of the value anyway. Complaint data and known use problems in week one. Cognitive task analysis with `[n]` expert surgeons through weeks two and four, anchored on remembered cases. Workflow mapping across roles. Then I'd deliver the draft use-related risk analysis and a first set of design inputs — so that when a prototype does exist, the evaluation is already scoped and the risk file isn't starting from zero."

### Senior → Staff-signal

- **Senior:** proposes a sound, well-sequenced research plan.
- **Staff-signal:** distinguishes generative from evaluative methods precisely, names the counterintuitive risk, and ties the early work to the regulatory record it becomes.
- **Say this:** *"Those early risk analysis entries are what the submission gets argued from, years later. I treat generative work as the first draft of the submission argument, not as pre-work."*

## MQ02 — From observation to design input ★ the most valuable thing you can say

**Asked by:** everyone · **Target:** ~85s · **Structure:** B

> **Base:** "How do you turn research into requirements?" / "What does your output look like?" / "How do you author usability design inputs?"

This is exactly what the job description means by *authoring usability design inputs*. Say the chain out loud, end to end.

### Model answer (~225 words / 90s)

**The claim.** "My output isn't a report, it's a design input an engineer can verify without me in the room. Let me walk the chain — and I'll make the numbers up, because the point is the shape.

**The chain.** *Observation:* surgeons tell me they're uncertain about grip on friable tissue — vessel wall, bowel — and they compensate by watching how it deforms. *Need:* the user has to be able to distinguish a safe grip from an unsafe one without taking their eyes off the operative field. *Design input:* the system shall present force information such that a one-newton difference is discriminable within a hundred and fifty milliseconds of contact, without requiring a visual fixation shift.

**And the risk analysis entry, in the same breath.** The hazard is mechanical force. The sequence of events: the surgeon can't perceive grip force, so grip escalates. The hazardous situation: a vessel or bowel wall exposed to force beyond what it tolerates. The harm: laceration or bleeding. The critical task is grip application during grasping and retraction. The candidate risk control is the force display, with its effectiveness verified in formative evaluation before the first integrated prototype review.

**The decision it drives.** That's the whole path, from something somebody said to something an engineer can test against — and to a traceable entry somebody can find three years later.

**What would change my mind.** If the discriminable difference turns out to be far outside what the hardware can deliver, the design input was written against the wrong control and I'd go back to the need, not relax the number."

> ⚠️ **Say "I'll make the numbers up" out loud.** It lets you be fully concrete without claiming to know their thresholds — and being concrete is the entire value of this answer.

### Follow-ups

**F1 — "Who owns that requirement once you've written it?"** *(~60 words)*

> "Human factors owns the claim about the user; systems owns whether it's achievable. The requirement is a joint artifact, and it's not done until an engineer agrees it's verifiable. A design input nobody can test against isn't a requirement, it's a recommendation with confident formatting."

**F2 — "What if engineering says that's not feasible?"** *(~65 words)*

> "Then we substitute a channel rather than relaxing the number, and the need stays fixed. If the haptic path can't deliver discriminability in time, the question becomes which other channel can carry the same information at acceptable cost. What degrades is completeness, not safety — and if nothing can carry it, that's a documented residual risk with a name against it, not an omission."

**F3 — "Where does the number itself come from?"** *(~65 words)*

> "Psychophysics, with the uncertainty carried forward. An adaptive threshold procedure converging on the detection point, the psychometric function fit per participant, and the specification takes the conservative bound of the interval rather than the mean. And you choose the population percentile deliberately and write down who it excludes — because a central-tendency spec silently designs for the median user and fails the tails."

### Senior → Staff-signal

- **Senior:** describes translating findings into recommendations.
- **Staff-signal:** produces the requirement in *shall* form and the risk analysis entry in the same answer, unprompted.
- **Say this:** *"A design input nobody can test against isn't a requirement, it's a recommendation with confident formatting."*

## MQ03 — Psychophysics into a specification

**Asked by:** engineers, senior HF · **Target:** ~85s · **Structure:** B

> **Base:** "Tell me about the latency work." / "How do you set a threshold?" / "How do you make a perceptual finding actionable?"

**The reframe.** You've told this as a requirements story. Here it's a *design input* story — a claim about human perception, measured properly and written so an engineer can test it.

### Model answer (~215 words / 86s)

**The claim.** "A latency target is a claim about human perception, so it should be measured like one and written down like one — not set as a round number somebody liked.

**How I'd know.** At Amazon, Alexa had latency targets that were essentially arbitrary. So we `[defined the measurement moment precisely / measured detection thresholds adaptively / carried the uncertainty into the spec / chose a population percentile deliberately]` — the measured interval wasn't 'system latency,' it was the gap between the person's input and the first feedback they get, in a named channel.

**Evidence, and where it stops.** What came out wasn't a report. It was an acceptance criterion an engineer could verify: feedback within this interval, for this share of users, under worst-case system load, with a defined fallback if the hardware can't get there. The economics team applied those thresholds and projected about fifty million dollars in operational value. The boundary is real, though: thresholds are specific to the channel and the workload. A visual feedback threshold from a consumer device does not port to a gloved, sterile, high-tempo console. The method ports. The number doesn't.

**The decision it drives.** It's the same shape as force feedback. How much force difference can a surgeon actually tell apart? At what delay does force stop feeling like it's coming from the instrument? What happens when the hardware can't deliver it? Those are psychophysics questions, and they all become design inputs and entries in the risk analysis.

**What would change my mind.** If users under realistic workload show no performance or trust difference across the threshold band, latency isn't the binding constraint and I'd re-derive it under load."

> ⚠️ **Keep only the methods you actually did.** Each of those four is a specific methodological claim inviting a different follow-up. Saying all four when you did two is how a good story becomes an audit.

### Follow-ups

**F1 — "What does 'worst-case system load' mean?"** *(~50 words)*

> "*System* load, not operator workload — the study held operator workload constant. That distinction matters, because a threshold measured on an unloaded operator won't hold for a loaded one, and I wouldn't want to imply we'd characterized both."

**F2 — "What percentile did you specify to, and who does that exclude?"** *(~65 words)*

> "`[percentile]`, and I write the exclusion down explicitly, because that's the part that gets lost. A central-tendency spec silently designs for the median user and fails the tails — and in a clinical setting the tails aren't edge cases, they're the surgeon at hour six of a difficult case. If I can't cover a tail, it becomes a documented residual risk rather than an omission."

**F3 — "What if the hardware can't hit the number?"** *(~70 words)*

> "You substitute a channel, you don't relax the number. If the visual path can't confirm in time, you acknowledge in a faster channel, so the person still feels the system responded while the real answer resolves. What degrades is completeness in that first window — they know the system heard them before they know what it decided. That's an acceptable trade. Silence isn't."

### Senior → Staff-signal

- **Senior:** reports a threshold and the study that produced it.
- **Staff-signal:** shows the population-to-specification translation, makes the tail decision explicit and owned, and volunteers the transfer boundary unprompted.
- **Say this:** *"The durable output wasn't the number. It was that latency arguments stopped being opinion — anyone proposing a target had to say which percentile and which channel."*

## MQ04 — Method selection and study scoping

**Asked by:** HF peers, PMs · **Target:** ~80s · **Structure:** B

> **Base:** "How do you choose a method?" / "How many participants?" / "How do you scope a formative study?" / "When is research not worth running?"

### Model answer (~205 words / 82s)

**The claim.** "Method follows the decision, not the other way round, and I work down a ladder — stopping at the cheapest rung that answers the question.

**How I'd know.** The first question is always what decision is pending and what would change it. If the answer is 'we want to understand the space,' that's generative: contextual inquiry, cognitive task analysis, critical decision method. If it's 'we think this design works,' that's evaluative and needs something to look at: cognitive walkthrough first because it's free, then formative testing at the lowest fidelity that can produce the error you're worried about. High fidelity is expensive and mostly buys confidence, not information.

**Evidence, and where it stops.** Sample size follows the same logic. For formative work, small and iterative beats large and single-shot — I'd rather run five participants three times than fifteen once, because the point is to change the design between rounds. Validation testing is a different animal: that's generally at least fifteen participants per distinct user group, representative users, production-equivalent interface, and deviations need justification. Those two things get confused constantly, and treating a formative study as a small validation is the most common way the record gets weak.

**The decision it drives.** Formative isn't a small summative. It's the only place the design actually changes.

**What would change my mind.** If the cheap method and the expensive one keep producing the same answer on a program, I stop buying the expensive one."

### Follow-ups

**F1 — "When would you not run a study at all?"** *(~55 words)*

> "When no plausible result changes a decision. If I walk the branches with the stakeholder and every outcome leads to the same action, the study is reassurance and I'd rather say so and give the time back. That's happened — `[one example]` — and it bought more credibility than the study would have."

**F2 — "How do you decide what's a critical task?"** *(~70 words)*

> "By the severity of the harm, not the probability. You can't estimate the probability of a use error reliably, which is exactly why the framework prioritizes on severity. I'd get there through a hierarchical task analysis — decompose the procedure, then ask of each step what happens to the patient if it goes wrong. Anything with an irreversible outcome is on the list regardless of how rare it looks."

**F3 — "What do you do with a use error you find late?"** *(~70 words)*

> "Work the hierarchy in order: can we design it out, can we protect against it, and only then do we talk about labeling or training. And you can't close out an observed use error with labeling or training alone unless you have evidence the fix works — which means testing the mitigation, not just adding it. A warning that nobody reads isn't a risk control, it's a record of having noticed."

### Senior → Staff-signal

- **Senior:** selects appropriate methods and justifies sample sizes.
- **Staff-signal:** separates formative from validation logic explicitly, and names the point at which a method becomes expensive theater.
- **Say this:** *"Formative isn't a small summative. It's the only place the design actually changes."*

## RQ01 — The 2026 guidance landscape

**Asked by:** regulatory/quality, senior HF · **Target:** ~80s · **Structure:** B

> **Base:** "What's your familiarity with the FDA human factors guidance?" / "What changed this year?"

### Model answer (~200 words / 80s)

**The claim.** "FDA refreshed the entire human factors framework inside a single year, and the most useful thing about it is that the two documents now work as a matched pair.

**The two documents.** The May one — *Content of Human Factors Information in Medical Device Marketing Submissions*, announced May 29 — tells you **what** goes in a submission, across 510(k)s, De Novo, PMA, and HDE. The August one — *Applying Human Factors and Usability Engineering to Medical Devices*, revised August 3 — tells you **how** to do the work. It's the first revision since 2016.

**What changed.** FDA's own change table lists three things. Definitions were updated and expanded — including broader definitions of *task* and *user interface*, and 'risk management measure' became **risk control measure** throughout. Section 9, the documentation section, got much shorter and now points at the May guidance. And Appendix A — the familiar eight-section report outline — was deleted, because it now lives in the May document.

**The decision it drives.** The date to know is August 1, 2026: submissions received before then aren't expected to include the newly recommended content.

**Why I find it interesting.** Teams that read only one of the two either do good work and present it badly, or present a tidy package built on a weak process."

### Follow-ups

**F1 — "What's the significance of the user interface definition change?"** *(~65 words)*

> "The user interface is now explicitly everything the user interacts through — packaging, labeling, training materials, controls, displays, alarms, and the device logic. The practical consequence catches people: labeling and training are *inside* the user interface, so a labeling-only change doesn't get you the lowest submission category. That's the trap I'd watch for."

**F2 — "What about QMSR?"** *(~50 words)*

> "The amended 21 CFR Part 820 incorporating ISO 13485 by reference, effective February 2 this year. The practical consequence for me is mostly vocabulary — it's the **design and development file** now, not the DHF. I'd defer to your quality team on the substantive process implications."

**F3 — "Are you a regulatory expert?"** *(~40 words)*

> "No, and I wouldn't claim to be. I'm a practitioner who has to produce records that survive that process, so I read the guidances carefully and I know where my knowledge stops. For anything past that I'd want regulatory in the room."

### Senior → Staff-signal

- **Senior:** knows the guidances exist and can summarize the changes.
- **Staff-signal:** explains why the pairing matters operationally, and volunteers the boundary of their own expertise without it reading as a weakness.
- **Say this:** *"One document is how you do the work, the other is what you hand over."*

## RQ02 — Submission categories and the judgment call

**Asked by:** regulatory/quality, senior HF · **Target:** ~80s · **Structure:** B

> **Base:** "Walk me through the submission categories." / "How do you decide whether validation testing is needed?"

### Model answer (~205 words / 82s)

**The claim.** "Every submission now lands in one of three categories, decided by a four-step flowchart — and the interesting part isn't the categories, it's Decision Point D.

**The three categories.** Category 1 is a modification with no change to the user interface, users, uses, use environments, training, or labeling — you submit a written justification that human factors is unaffected. Category 2 is either no critical tasks, or the modification doesn't touch any — *or* critical tasks are affected but validation testing isn't warranted. That gets a written rationale plus a defined subset of the report, and for a modified device, a comparative risk analysis. Category 3 is new or affected critical tasks with validation warranted: all eight sections, full risk analysis, critical task list, and the complete validation study with protocol attached.

**Decision Point D is the one worth knowing cold.** It's where you decide whether affected critical tasks still need validation testing, and the rationale rests on three things: an established history of safe use for that interface, low user interface complexity, and the continued adequacy of existing risk controls.

**The decision it drives.** That accommodation is new in the final — the 2022 draft didn't offer it. And it's exactly the judgment a senior analyst gets handed, because all three factors are arguable.

**What would change my mind.** If any of the three is weak, I'd rather run the validation than defend a rationale at review."

### Follow-ups

**F1 — "What's the trap people fall into?"** *(~60 words)*

> "Two. Labeling and training sit inside the user interface, so a labeling-only change doesn't get you Category 1 — people get that wrong constantly. And expanding an indication sends you back through the flowchart even with no device change at all; with a new user population it'll often land in Category 3."

**F2 — "How would you handle a borderline Category 2 versus 3 call?"** *(~55 words)*

> "A pre-submission. That's what the route is for, and the cost of asking is far lower than the cost of being wrong at review. I'd bring the risk analysis and the specific Decision Point D rationale, and ask whether the history-of-safe-use argument holds for that interface."

**F3 — "Who makes that call at a company?"** *(~50 words)*

> "Not me alone. Human factors owns the risk analysis and the critical task determination that the call rests on; regulatory owns the submission strategy. My job is to make the underlying evidence strong enough that the call is defensible either way — and to say clearly when I think it's close."

### Senior → Staff-signal

- **Senior:** can describe the categories accurately.
- **Staff-signal:** identifies the judgment call inside the framework, names all three factors behind it, and defaults to the conservative path when the rationale is thin.
- **Say this:** *"All three factors are arguable, which is exactly why it's the judgment a senior analyst gets handed."*

## RQ03 — The risk framework, and what didn't change

**Asked by:** regulatory, engineers, senior HF · **Target:** ~80s · **Structure:** B

> **Base:** "Walk me through use-related risk analysis." / "How do 62366 and 14971 fit together?" / "What's your risk analysis experience?"

**Anyone can recite what's new. Knowing what held is the tell that you understand the framework.**

### Model answer (~210 words / 84s)

**The claim.** "The load-bearing logic didn't change: critical tasks are prioritized by the severity of the potential harm, not by probability — because the probability of a use error can't be estimated reliably. That one sentence holds up the entire framework.

**The process chain, from 62366.** Use specification, then user interface characteristics related to safety, then known use problems, then hazard-related use scenarios, then critical tasks, then the evaluation plan, then formative evaluation, then validation. The chain *is* the traceability argument — every critical task has to have come from somewhere, and every risk control has to get evaluated.

**The causal chain, from 14971.** Hazard, then sequence of events, then **hazardous situation**, then **harm** — in that order. Most people collapse hazard and hazardous situation; not collapsing them is an instant credibility marker. And the control hierarchy is design it out, protect against it, tell people about it.

**The decision it drives.** Validation expectations held too: generally at least fifteen participants per distinct user group, representative US users, production-equivalent interface with final labeling, and no closing out an observed use error with labeling or training alone without evidence the fix works. Deviations need justification.

**Why it matters for my actual job.** The submission category and the validation argument get settled years downstream — but they're settled *by* the risk analysis somebody created at the very beginning. So I treat early generative work as the first draft of the submission argument, not as pre-work."

### Follow-ups

**F1 — "Give me a concrete hazard chain."** *(~70 words)*

> "Hazard: mechanical force. Sequence of events: the surgeon can't perceive grip force, so grip escalates. Hazardous situation: a vessel or bowel wall exposed to force beyond what it tolerates. Harm: laceration or bleeding. Critical task: grip application during grasping and retraction. Candidate risk control: the force display — with its effectiveness verified in formative evaluation, not assumed."

**F2 — "Where does human factors sit relative to the risk file?"** *(~60 words)*

> "Human factors owns the use-related portion — the use errors, the hazard-related use scenarios, the critical task list, and the evidence that the risk controls work. That feeds the broader risk management file that systems and quality own. The failure mode is those two living separately, where a use-related control gets credited without anyone testing whether users actually perceive it."

**F3 — "What makes a risk analysis weak?"** *(~60 words)*

> "Critical tasks that appear without traceable derivation, and risk controls credited without evaluation. If I can't walk backwards from a critical task to the use scenario and the known use problem that produced it, the chain is broken — and that's the thing that's expensive to fix three years later, when the argument has to be made from the record."

### Senior → Staff-signal

- **Senior:** knows the standards and can describe the process.
- **Staff-signal:** leads with what *didn't* change, keeps hazard and hazardous situation distinct without prompting, and connects the early record to the downstream submission argument.
- **Say this:** *"Critical tasks are prioritized by the severity of the harm, not the probability — because the probability of a use error can't be estimated reliably."*
