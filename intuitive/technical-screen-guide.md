## SQ03 — Defining a workflow ★ named directly in the track description

**Asked by:** clinical engineers, anyone · **Target:** ~85s · **Structure:** B

> **Base:** "How do you define a user workflow?" / "How would you map a procedure?" / "What does workflow definition actually produce?"

*User workflow definition* is one of the four things Reema listed for this track. Have a real answer, not a gesture at task analysis.

### Model answer (~210 words / 85s)

**The claim.** "A workflow definition is a design artifact, not documentation. If it only describes what people do today, it can't tell you what a new system has to support — so I build it to be argued with.

**How I'd know.** It starts from observation and cognitive task analysis, not from a document. Then a hierarchical decomposition, taken to the level where a single step can fail. Three things get annotated on top of the steps, and they're where the value is. Who holds what state at each moment. Where control or attention hands off between roles. And where the path goes off-nominal — because the normal case is the easy half, and the interesting failures live in recovery.

**Evidence, and where it stops.** I've built these for multi-role systems: the clinical task set on the NASA workstation, and across the Echo portfolio where several people share one device with different intents. The limit is real — I haven't mapped a full surgical procedure with a sterile field and a five-person team. I'd expect the synchronization points and the off-nominals to be where my first draft is wrong, which is why I'd walk it with the bedside staff and not just the console surgeon.

**The decision it drives.** The workflow is what you write design inputs against. It tells you which steps are critical tasks, which transitions need state to be perceptible, and where a new capability has to fit without displacing something already load-bearing.

**What would change my mind.** If observation shows the team routinely runs a path my decomposition doesn't contain, the map is wrong. I rebuild it from observation rather than patch it."

## MQ02 — From observation to design input ★ the most valuable thing you say all day

**Asked by:** systems engineers, HF peers · **Target:** ~90s · **Structure:** B

*Interface design requirements* is the second thing Reema named. Say the chain end to end, with numbers — being concrete is the entire value of this answer.

### Model answer (~225 words / 90s)

**The claim.** "My output isn't a report, it's a design input an engineer can verify without me in the room. Take a hypothetical new grasper for friable tissue — I'll make the numbers up, because the point is the shape.

**The chain.** *Observation:* surgeons tell me they're uncertain about grip on friable tissue — vessel wall, bowel — and compensate by watching how it deforms. *Need:* the user has to distinguish a safe grip from an unsafe one at the moment of application, not after the tissue responds. *Design input:* the system shall present force information such that a one-newton difference is discriminable within a hundred and fifty milliseconds of contact.

**And the risk analysis entry, in the same breath.** Hazard: mechanical force. Sequence of events: the surgeon can't perceive grip force, so grip escalates. **The decision it drives.** That's the whole path — from something somebody said, to something an engineer can test against, to a traceable entry somebody can find three years later.

**What would change my mind.** If the discriminable difference turns out far outside what the hardware can deliver, the design input was written against the wrong control, and I'd go back to the need rather than relax the number."

## SQ04 — Prototyping and fidelity ★ early prototype development, in one story

**Asked by:** designers, anyone · **Target:** ~80s · **Structure:** B

> **Base:** "How do you decide what to prototype?" / "What fidelity do you build?" / "Tell me about a rig you built."

### Model answer (~205 words / 82s)

**The claim.** "Fidelity is a decision variable, not a quality level. You build the cheapest thing that can produce the specific answer you need, and deliberately leave everything else rough.

**How I'd know — the clearest example I have.** On the Alexa latency program I needed per-trial millisecond control of response delay across twelve interaction types. A real system couldn't give me that: its latency was a distribution, not a setting. **What would change my mind.** If participants behave differently when they suspect a person is in the loop, the rig is measuring compliance rather than perception, and it has to be replaced with an instrumented real system."

**F1 — "What's the earliest thing you'd put in front of a surgeon?"** *(~60 words)*

You learn whether you've understood the task before you've spent anything on an interface, and a surgeon will correct a wrong sequence far faster than they'll critique a screen."

### The Staff-signal

- **Staff-signal:** treats fidelity as lopsided on purpose, names the decision the rig served, and flags the condition under which the method breaks.
- **Say this:** *"Exact where the question lives, near-zero effort everywhere else."*

## MQ01 — The research hypothetical ★ and the fact that force feedback already ships

**Asked by:** anyone, especially clinical engineers · **Target:** ~105s · **Structure:** B

> **Base:** "How would you approach research for force feedback on a surgical system?" / any "how would you research X" hypothetical.

> ⚠️ **Force feedback is not hypothetical, and they built it.** It shipped on **da Vinci 5** in the US in **March 2024** — tip sensors relaying push and pull forces to the console hand controllers — and the **Force Gauge**, a visual indicator of relative tip force from **0 to 6.5 newtons**, followed on **September 12, 2025** (confirmed on Intuitive's investor newsroom). Treating this as greenfield tells them you don't know their flagship.

**Announce the order after the claim:** *"Let me take that in order: what's known, what I'd ask experts, what I'd test, and what it turns into."*

### Model answer (~245 words / 100s)

**The claim.** "Force feedback already ships on da Vinci 5, alongside a visual gauge. So the question isn't *whether* force information helps. It's where each channel earns its place — which tasks, what happens when the haptic and visual cues disagree, and how experienced surgeons adapt. I'll take it as extending it to a new instrument or procedure.

**What's already known.** Complaint and adverse-event data on the fielded system — internal records plus FDA's MAUDE database. It's free evidence and the cheapest hypotheses I'll get.

**What I'd ask experts.** Cognitive task analysis with expert surgeons about how they work *now*: which cues they actually use, where the gauge earns a glance, when force information changed a decision, where they still feel blind. Anchored in real remembered cases. And workflow mapping across the whole team, because the bedside assistant and the scrub are users too.

**What I'd test.** Once there's something to react to: cognitive walkthroughs, then formative testing at rising fidelity, each round tied to a decision agreed in advance.

**The decision it drives.** Verifiable design inputs — a detectable force difference, a latency bound, a defined behavior when feedback drops out — plus entries in the use-related risk analysis.

**What would change my mind.** If experienced users still rely on the visual strategy and mostly ignore the force channel, the work isn't tuning the signal. It's understanding why a shipped capability isn't being used, and that's a different study."

### Follow-ups

**F1 — "What are the risks of adding force feedback?"** *(~95 words)*

> "Six, and on a shipped capability they matter more. The one people miss is negative transfer. Experienced robotic surgeons taught themselves to read force visually, so a new force cue could get in the way of that skill at first. I'd test whether experienced surgeons show negative transfer early — that's a hypothesis to check, not something to assume. The others: haptic and visual cues disagreeing, which is worse than either alone; latency, where late force stops feeling like it came from the instrument; over-trust, where visual cross-checking fades; habituation; and losing feedback mid-case, which is an unannounced mode change."

**F2 — "Six weeks, no prototype. What do you do?"** *(~75 words)*

> "Everything upstream of the prototype, which is most of the value anyway. Complaint data and known use problems in week one. Cognitive task analysis with eight to twelve expert surgeons through weeks two to four, anchored on remembered cases. Workflow mapping across roles. Then I'd deliver the draft use-related risk analysis and a first set of design inputs — so when a prototype exists the evaluation is already scoped and the risk file isn't starting from zero."

**F3 — "What would you compare against?"** *(~60 words)*

> "Three reference points. Open surgery, where the surgeon feels tissue directly. Laparoscopic, where force comes through a long instrument and a pivot, dulled and distorted. And robotic practice before force feedback, where surgeons learned to read force visually. That third group is the interesting one — they built a skill the hardware now partly replaces."

> ⚠️ **One precision point, and both of them will hear it.** Cognitive task analysis studies how experts think in current practice, needs no prototype, and **generates.** Cognitive walkthroughs and formative testing need something to look at, and **evaluate.** Using them interchangeably tells a specialist you learned the vocabulary rather than the practice.

### The Staff-signal

- **Staff-signal:** separates generative from evaluative methods precisely, names the counterintuitive risk, and ties early work to the regulatory record it becomes.
- **Say this:** *"I treat generative work as the first draft of the submission argument, not as pre-work."*

## SQ11 — Multimodal channel allocation ★ one of your three "why here" reasons

**Asked by:** systems engineers, HF peers · **Target:** ~85s · **Structure:** B

> **Base:** "How do you decide whether information should be visual, auditory, or haptic?" / "When would you add a second channel?" / "What's the risk of redundant cues?"

You name multimodal as a reason you're here. This is the answer that backs it up.

### Model answer (~215 words / 85s)

**The claim.** "Channel choice follows the primary task. The question is what's already loaded, not which channel is best.

**How I'd know.** A simple rule of thumb. Vision carries spatial and relational information — where things are and how they relate. Sound carries onset and urgency — something just changed, and how much it matters. Touch carries continuous state and confirmation — how hard, and did it take. Underneath that is multiple resource theory: when the primary task saturates one channel, a second channel still has capacity.

**The risk.** Redundant channels that disagree are worse than one channel alone. So the design has to say, in advance, which one the user should trust.

**The decision it drives.** A design input that names the primary channel, the redundant channel, and the defined behavior when they conflict or one drops out.

**Where it stops.** The console surgeon is visually isolated from the room, so the auditory and haptic assumptions carry a lot of weight. I'd test them in the actual room, with alarms and noise — not in a quiet lab."

### Follow-ups

**F1 — "What if the two channels disagree during a case?"** *(~50 words)*

> "Then the design should already have decided which one wins, and it should tell the user they disagree. How often it happens, and what users actually do when it does, is a formative question. I'd measure it rather than guess."

### The Staff-signal

- **Staff-signal:** allocates by load, not preference, and specifies the conflict behavior, not just the channels.

## If they hand you a scenario or a device

Same six steps as MQ04, said out loud before you start — naming the method is half the signal.
1. **Users, uses, environment.** Who touches it, which procedure phase, sterile or non-sterile.
2. **Task decomposition.** Steps at a level where each one can fail.
3. **Failure modes per step.** For each step: can the user perceive it, understand it, and act on it correctly? Then omission, wrong sequence, wrong object, wrong timing.
4. **Critical tasks, by severity alone.** If doing it wrong, or not at all, could cause serious harm, it's critical — however unlikely the error.
5. **Controls and design inputs.** Design it out, protect against it, inform last — each a verifiable *shall*.
6. **Evaluation.** What formative test would show the control works.

> ⚠️ **Narrate as an analyst, not an enthusiast.** Observe and ask; don't perform. If you don't know how something works, say so and ask — guessing at a mechanism in front of the people who built it is the one unrecoverable move.

## EQ08 — Design partner, not design grader ★ this is what "design engineer" means here

**Asked by:** designers, product · **Target:** ~80s · **Structure:** A

### Model answer (~205 words / 82s)

**Situation.** "At EchoStar I report to the VP of Product and lead human factors across software, physical hardware, and platform integrations. There was no function before me, so every bit of influence had to be earned rather than assigned.

**Task.** My charge was to make human factors something teams wanted early rather than something that happened to them late.

**Action.** The move that worked was bringing constraints instead of verdicts. Rather than reviewing a design and listing problems, I'd arrive at concept stage with the envelope — reach and anatomical safety limits for hardware, perceptual thresholds for latency and feedback, criteria for screen layout. On one hardware program I brought the reach envelope into the concept review rather than the design review, and a control placement moved before any tooling existed — cheap then, expensive three months later. **Earned secret.** Human factors gets treated as a gate when it arrives late and as a partner when it arrives early — and that's almost entirely within my control, not theirs.

**F1 — "What if a designer pushes back on your constraint?"** *(~60 words)*

> "Then I need to be clear about which kind of constraint it is. Some are perceptual or anatomical facts and aren't negotiable. Others are my judgment about a tradeoff, and those genuinely are. **F2 — "How do you work with engineers versus designers?"** *(~60 words)*

## MQ04 — Use-error analysis and residual risk ★ your closest analogue

**Asked by:** HF peers, systems · **Target:** ~100s · **Structure:** B

> **Base:** "Walk me through how you'd do a use-related risk analysis." / "What's your uFMEA experience?" / "When is residual risk acceptable?" / "Tell me about work where the consequences were serious."

Design Engineering owns this analysis up to the validation handoff, and it carries your NASA story — so don't tell that story twice.

> **Any risk question opens here:** *"Acceptability is defined in the risk management plan before anything is analyzed. I drive each risk down the control hierarchy, verify the controls work, and weigh what remains against clinical benefit."*

### Model answer (~250 words / 100s)

**The claim.** "Acceptability is defined in the risk management plan before anything is analyzed. Then I drive each risk down the control hierarchy, verify the controls work, and weigh what remains against clinical benefit. The analysis is where the design gets decided, not where it gets checked.

**What counts as critical.** A critical task is one where doing it wrong, or not doing it at all, could cause serious harm. I decide that by severity alone, not by how likely the error is.

**How I'd know.** I work from both ends. Top-down: start from the harms and work back to the interactions that cause them. Bottom-up: a usability FMEA. Break the procedure into steps, and for each one ask: can the user perceive it, understand it, and act on it correctly? A failure at any of the three is a use-error mode. Where the two directions disagree is usually where the real problem is.

**Evidence, and where it stops.** I did this at NASA Langley on the Lunar Gateway medical workstation, where a crew member gets treated with no evacuation. I derived the task list and ran the uFMEA. The worst failure modes came from adjacent controls with different consequences, so we separated them over three to four iterations, against NASA-STD-3001 and MIL-STD-1472. No critical input errors in the final round *[CONFIRM: N]*; task time fell about thirty percent as a side effect. That's formative evidence on a small sample, not a validation result.

**The decision it drives.** The critical task list plus controls, each traced to its scenario. I changed the layout rather than adding a warning, because a label is the weakest control there is."

> ⚠️ **The old résumé's NASA line said "design validation."** If anyone raises it: *"That was formative simulated-use evaluation in the NASA sense — not HF validation testing in the FDA sense."*

### Follow-ups

**F1 — "How do you keep it from becoming a spreadsheet nobody reads?"** *(~40 words)*

> "I never shrink the analysis. Every task stays in the file. What I shrink is the list people act on: the critical tasks. The file stays complete; the conversation stays focused."

**F2 — "How do you handle a risk control that's also a use-error source?"** *(~60 words)*

> "You analyze it like any other interface element, because it is one. A confirmation step added to prevent one error creates its own failure modes — habituation, and the error of confirming reflexively. So a risk control isn't finished when it's specified. It's finished when it's been evaluated for the errors it introduces as well as the ones it prevents."

**F3 — "What if a risk is still too high after the controls?"** *(~60 words)*

> "Then it goes back up the hierarchy first — can we design it out, or add a protective measure — before anything lands on a label. If what remains still doesn't meet the plan's criteria, it goes to a benefit-risk decision on the record, with a name against it. My job is making sure nothing is residual by omission."

> ⚠️ **Never frame acceptance as a negotiation, or "not my decision."** The rule is pre-set criteria, a living risk file, and mitigation through design first. And never call a severity cutoff "arbitrary."

### The Staff-signal

- **Staff-signal:** opens on the plan's criteria, defines critical by severity in one sentence, and names the control hierarchy as the *reason* for the design choice.
- **Say this:** *"I changed the layout rather than adding a warning. A label is the weakest control there is."*

## SQ10 — HF validation, done right ★ new since the technical round

**Asked by:** HF peers, systems · **Target:** ~80s · **Structure:** B

> **Base:** "What makes a good validation study?" / "Walk me through HF validation testing." / "How is validation different from formative?"

You don't run validation here, but you have to know exactly what goes into it. Get this wrong and nothing upstream sounds credible.

### Model answer (~195 words / 80s)

**The claim.** "Validation answers one question: can representative users perform the critical tasks safely with the final design?

**How it's set up.** A production-equivalent device, a realistic use environment, and fifteen or more participants per distinct user group — a console surgeon and a scrub are different groups. They get the training and labeling they'd actually get. Nothing extra.

**What it isn't.** It's not a comparison of designs, and it's not a statistics exercise. Nobody is chasing an effect size. It's a structured search for use errors on the tasks that can hurt someone.

**How I'd know.** I watch every critical task for use errors, close calls, and difficulties. Then I interview each participant to find the root cause. Tasks you can't observe — like understanding a warning — get knowledge questions.

**The decision it drives.** If a failure traces back to the design, it's a design fix, not a caveat in the report.

**Where I sit.** That's why upstream matters. If my critical tasks and scenarios are right, validation confirms the design instead of discovering problems in it."

### Follow-ups

**F1 — "How is formative different?"** *(~45 words)*

> "Formative is small and repeated — about five people, three times, not fifteen once — because the point is to change the design between rounds. A formative finding justifies a design decision, not a safety claim."

**F2 — "One participant makes a critical use error. Now what?"** *(~55 words)*

> "Find the root cause before deciding anything. If it's the design, fix the design and show the fix works. If it's genuinely a study artifact, document why, carefully — because 'the participant was confused' is the answer that sounds most like an excuse."

### The Staff-signal

- **Staff-signal:** states the one question validation answers, and ties its success to upstream work.
- **Say this:** *"Validation should confirm the design, not discover problems in it."*

## EQ06 — When you got it wrong

**Asked by:** anyone, especially product · **Target:** ~75s · **Structure:** A

> **Base:** "Tell me about a study that didn't go as planned." / "When has your research been wrong?"

> ⚠️ **Do not use the graduate task-resumption study here.** *[CONFIRM: is the 46-of-51 null the laparoscopic thesis? If so, delete it.]* The thesis produced **real findings** — calling it a null is the most catchable contradiction you could make.

### Model answer (~175 words / 72s)

**Situation.** "My first project at Amazon. I benchmarked Echo Show against competitors on a set of high-priority tasks. Nobody commissioned it — I recruited the participants myself and spent two weeks on it.

**Result.** At the readout, the director of design asked who had asked for it. I had no answer. The roadmap was already set, and the work changed nothing.

**Earned secret.** Initiative without a pre-agreed decision is just spend. The study was competent and the finding was real, which is exactly what made it a useful failure — rigor wasn't the missing part.

**Mechanism.** So before any study runs now, stakeholders commit in writing to what each possible result will change. If every branch leads to the same design, the study doesn't run — and that rule has killed proposals of mine.

**Where it transfers.** In a regulated setting the same discipline is what keeps formative work honest: a formative study exists to change a design decision. If it can't, it isn't evidence, it's reassurance."

### Follow-ups

**F1 — "How do you get stakeholders to actually commit to that?"** *(~60 words)*

> "I write the branches out as a one-page pre-read — if the result is A we do this, if it's B we do that. It's much harder to stay vague in writing than in a meeting. And if nobody will sign any branch, that's the answer: the decision isn't actually open, so the study would be reassurance."

> ⚠️ **Graduate work gets one beat and no more.** Thesis is laparoscopy, 2017, with medical students; dissertation is medical data entry, 2020; never backwards. **Only if an interviewer raises OR disruptions first**, cite Catchpole or Weigl as field literature: most disruptions don't stop the task, they degrade it.

## Held in reserve — the spine only

**Complaint data and known use problems.** *"Before any new study, I start with complaint and adverse-event data on the predecessor device — internal records plus FDA's MAUDE database."* A complaint is written in the language of outcome; my job is to reconstruct the use scenario that produced it. Severity first, then cluster by scenario rather than symptom. Hypothesis-generating, not rate-estimating.

**Personas and user groups.** *"A persona is the decision about who counts as a distinct user group — and that decision sets validation sampling and who each critical task belongs to."* Here the groups are role-based — console surgeon, bedside assistant, scrub, circulator — not demographic.
