Intuitive Surgical — Senior Human Factors Analyst

Interview guide · Sunnyvale · fully onsite

Hiring director conversation held September 15. It went well. What's left is the stakeholder calls and the onsite loop.

Parts 1–3 are eighteen questions to rehearse out loud. Each has the question as it will actually be asked, a model answer, three scoped follow-ups, and the line that separates Senior from Staff-signal. Parts 4–6 are reference — read once, don't memorize.

How the page works. Blue = the question as they'll ask it. Dark-bordered = the model answer, the thing you rehearse. Grey = a follow-up answer. Yellow = a warning; read every one.

One answer per question — however they word it. Interviewers rephrase. The claim, the evidence, and the boundary stay identical in every room, because they compare notes afterward.

Three answers are marked CONFIRM — reconstructed from your résumé rather than dictated by you. Correct them before you say them.

Two structures. Using the wrong one costs you even when the content is right.

Every question is tagged A or B. Tell a story in answer to a method question and it reads as dodging; recite method in answer to an experience question and it reads as evasive.

Structure A — experience, behavioral, cross-functional

Beat Words What it must do
Situation 20–30 One sentence. Where, the stakes, why it was hard.
Task 15–25 Your specific charge, in first person.
Action 90–120 "I," not "we." The hardest moment is the centerpiece. Name what you decided against.
Result 25–40 Quantified where the evidence supports it. Attribution honest.
Earned secret 20–30 The thing you know that most people in your role don't.
Mechanism 20–30 The standard, gate, or artifact you left behind.

200–265 words ≈ 90–110 seconds. The mechanism beat is the gate between Senior and Staff. Say it as its own sentence: "What I left behind was X, and Y still has to clear it."

Structure B — methodological, technical, regulatory

Beat Words What it must do
The claim 20–30 Lead with the position. No windup.
How I'd know 40–60 The method, or how the effect actually works.
Evidence, and where it stops 40–60 Your evidence, then its boundary — before anyone asks.
The decision it drives 30–45 The design input, threshold, or gate this produces.
What would change my mind 20–35 The result that would overturn the claim.

160–230 words ≈ 65–90 seconds. Follow-ups: 45–90 words ≈ 20–35 seconds.

The register check

"We ran a study and reported findings" is baseline. "I set the perceptual threshold that latency budgets had to clear" is the level you're interviewing at. Hedges — "I sort of drove," "they let me," "I was involved in" — cap you regardless of how good the work was. Delete them for the day.

Asked by: everyone, first · Target: ~75s · Structure: special, not STAR

Model answer (~185 words / 75s)

What I do. "I turn human perceptual and cognitive limits into design inputs that engineers can verify — in systems where a use error causes physical harm.

Why here, three reasons. First, I want the consequence back. I do my best work when a use error means physical harm, not a bad quarter. Second, the problem is multimodal — visual, haptic, and auditory interaction under load is what I've spent my career measuring. The arc. Depth, then breadth, then building. Four and a half years at Amazon as the sole human factors researcher in the Devices Design Group. Then consulting. EQ02 — The unarticulated need ★ rehearse this one hardest

Asked by: hiring manager, senior HF, designers · Target: ~95s · Structure: A

Base: "Tell me about a time you identified a user need nobody had articulated." / "How have you worked in ambiguity?" / "Tell me about 0→1 research."

This is the center of gravity for the role. The job description is almost entirely formative work, defining needs, and authoring design inputs.

⚠️ CONFIRM this whole answer against what actually happened. It's reconstructed from your résumé and from what Echo Hub actually shipped — the shape is right and the details are plausible, but a panel will probe them and you need them to be true.

Model answer (~240 words / 95s)

Situation. "Echo Hub was a genuinely new kind of device — a control panel mounted on a wall and shared by a whole household. No prior product to test against, no usage data, nothing to run a study on. I was the only human factors researcher in the group.

Task. My charge was to define what it needed to do for people before there was a design to evaluate. And the first question wasn't about the interface at all — it was what a wall-mounted panel has to show someone who is walking past it, rather than standing at it.

Action. I ran in-home contextual inquiry with twelve households across a range of smart-home density, watching how people actually controlled their connected devices over a normal week. What came out was that nobody wanted a faster controller. They wanted to not have to decide whether crossing the room was worth it. The reason nobody had written that down is that the workaround looked like ordinary behavior — people reached for their phone even when the panel was closer, and then told us the panel was great. A single qualitative source isn't enough to move an architecture decision, so I checked it against the existing Echo Show interaction data, which showed the same pattern: a fixed-position device gets used for whatever is already on screen, not for what's one navigation step away. Then I translated it into something an engineer could verify rather than interpret: the device shall resolve to the controls relevant to that room and that time of day before the user reaches it, so the panel is readable in a single glance at approach distance without a touch to orient.

Result. That shaped the decision to treat approach and proximity as a first-class input to what the screen shows, rather than treating the panel as a small wall-mounted tablet.

Earned secret. The needs that don't get written down are the ones where the workaround looks like ordinary behavior. Nobody reports it as a problem, because from the inside it isn't one.

Mechanism. What I left behind was the glance-first requirement and the criteria behind it, and the Echo hardware and Alexa interface teams still design against it."

Follow-ups

F1 — "How did you know that was real and not just a few people talking?" (~70 words)

"Two things. One, I don't move an architecture decision on a single qualitative source, so I looked for it in the existing Echo Show interaction data — different method, same conclusion. Two, I was looking for a compensating behavior rather than a stated preference. What people say they want is unreliable; what they've already built a workaround for is evidence, because they paid a cost for it without being asked."

F2 — "Why should that transfer here? It's a consumer product." (~70 words)

"The limit is real: it's a household, and there's no clinical consequence when you get it wrong. The method transfers, the stakes don't. But structurally it's the same problem as a new console capability — no fielded product to test against, several roles sharing one system, each with seconds of attention to spare. You have to find the need in the environment before you have anything to evaluate."

F3 — "What were the adoption numbers?" (~45 words)

"I'd be careful with specifics there, but at the portfolio level it changed how the device decides what to show before you touch it. I'd rather give you the reasoning and the requirement than a figure I shouldn't be repeating."

⚠️ Portfolio level only. No Echo Hub or Echo Show metric. Refusing well reads as discipline to a medical device company — the same instinct they want you to have about their data.

Senior → Staff-signal

  • Senior: finds an insight and reports it persuasively.
  • Staff-signal: explains why the need was structurally invisible, corroborates across sources, and converts it into a shall statement someone else can verify.
  • Say this: "The needs that don't get written down are the ones where the workaround looks like ordinary behavior."

EQ03 — Pushing back

Asked by: senior HF, engineers, PMs · Target: ~95s · Structure: A

Base: "Tell me about a time you disagreed with engineering." / "How do you handle it when your recommendation is rejected?" / The job description says negotiate design recommendations — expect this.

⚠️ CONFIRM the specifics. This is built from the latency program, where your résumé says you replaced arbitrary engineering targets — so a real disagreement is implied. Correct the details to what actually happened.

Model answer (~235 words / 95s)

Situation. "On the Alexa latency program I recommended we stop using the existing engineering latency targets and replace them with perception-derived thresholds. For several interaction types that meant a tighter budget than engineering had been building to, so it carried real cost in effort and schedule — and the engineering leads who owned those budgets pushed back.

Task. My job was to either move the decision or accept it honestly, not to be right in a meeting.

Action. I granted the objection out loud first, because it was legitimate: they had been hitting their targets, those targets had been stable for years, and nothing was visibly broken. I was proposing to make their work harder on the strength of a research finding. Then I argued from the strongest ground I had, in order. First, that the existing numbers weren't a measurement of anything — they were a convention, and nobody could say what they were a threshold for. Second, converging evidence: the detection thresholds and the subjective slowness ratings pointed the same way. Third — the part that actually moved it — I built them a cheaper alternative: a two-tier specification per interaction type, a must-hit threshold and a target threshold, so they could sequence the work instead of absorbing every budget at once. I didn't escalate. Escalation is available, but it spends credit you need later and makes the next disagreement harder.

Result. The two-tier spec was adopted. What I conceded was the rollout sequence — lower-frequency interaction types kept their old budgets for a release cycle. If I'd conceded nothing, I wasn't negotiating, I was waiting.

Earned secret. I don't win these by being right. I win them by making the residual risk visible enough that accepting it requires a signature. Once a decision has a name attached, the conversation changes character.

Mechanism. What I left behind was the rule that any proposed latency target had to name its interaction type and the perceptual basis for the number — so the argument didn't recur on the next program."

Follow-ups

F1 — "What did you concede?" (~50 words)

"The rollout sequence. I gave ground on timing for the lower-frequency interactions, because the evidence there was genuinely thinner — fewer trials, wider interval. Holding every position equally hard is how you get treated as someone to route around rather than consult."

F2 — "What if they'd said no anyway?" (~60 words)

"Then it becomes a documented residual risk with an owner's name on it, and I move on without relitigating. What I don't do is quietly agree, or keep raising it in side channels. The record is the mechanism — if the risk materializes later, the analysis shows it was identified, quantified, and accepted deliberately."

F3 — "How do you avoid becoming the team's obstacle?" (~65 words)

"By showing up early enough that I'm shaping the design rather than grading it. Most friction in this job comes from human factors arriving after the architecture is fixed, when every finding is expensive. If I'm in at concept, my constraint is one input among several instead of a late veto — and I'm arguing with a whiteboard, not a schedule."

Senior → Staff-signal

  • Senior: advocates effectively with evidence.
  • Staff-signal: grants the counter-argument, names the concession, and converts a won argument into a standing gate.
  • Say this: "I don't win these by being right. I win them by making the residual risk visible enough that accepting it requires a signature."

EQ04 — Getting research acted on ★ your signature habit

Asked by: PMs, senior HF, hiring manager · Target: ~85s · Structure: A

Base: "How do you make sure your research actually gets used?" / "Tell me about influencing without authority." / "What do you do when findings get ignored?"

Model answer (~220 words / 88s)

Situation. "Early on I watched good studies land in a findings meeting and change nothing, and I stopped believing the problem was research quality or how well I presented it.

Task. So I changed what I do before a study runs rather than after.

Action. I get stakeholders to agree in writing on what each possible result will change. Not 'we'll consider the findings' — the actual branches. On the Alexa latency program that looked like this: if the measured threshold came in tighter than the current target, the target changes and engineering absorbs the work. If it came in looser, we stop spending engineering cycles on latency and move that effort to feature work. Both branches, written down, before anyone had a result to defend.

Result. The thresholds went to Alexa Economics, who applied them to their Negative Value Action model and projected about fifty million dollars in incremental operational value over fifteen months. Nobody relitigated whether the research counted, because that argument had already happened.

Earned secret. Findings don't get ignored because they're weak. They get ignored because nobody ever owned the decision they were meant to inform. Pre-commitment moves the argument to a moment when nobody's ego is attached to an outcome yet.

Mechanism. And it cuts both ways — it kills my own studies. I killed a proposed study on response phrasing once, because I walked the branches with the team and every outcome led to the same design. If no plausible result changes a decision, I don't run it."

⚠️ CONFIRM the cancelled study. The pre-commitment habit and the $50M outcome are yours; the phrasing study is a placeholder for whatever you actually killed. This line is what makes the answer read as judgment rather than process, so it has to be real and concrete.

Follow-ups

F1 — "What if a stakeholder won't pre-commit?" (~65 words)

"That's useful information, and I treat it as a scoping finding rather than an obstacle. Usually it means one of two things: the decision isn't actually theirs, so I'm talking to the wrong person, or there's no real decision pending and the study is reassurance. Either way I'd rather find out before I spend six weeks than after."

F2 — "How does that work against a development gate?" (~65 words)

"It maps cleanly. The formative plan says: if people fail this task at this rate, the design changes — and here's the alternative we've already scoped. That turns human factors from a reporting function into something that holds a gate, and it makes the research schedule predictable, because decision points are named before the work starts."

F3 — "Give me a time you changed an organization's language, not just a decision." (~70 words)

"On the latency program the metric everyone used conflated the objective response interval with the user's perception of it — the name itself encoded an assumption my data falsified. I proposed splitting them, and the organization renamed the metric. That mattered more than any single threshold, because afterwards nobody could argue the two were the same thing by accident."

Senior → Staff-signal

  • Senior: communicates findings clearly and builds relationships to drive adoption.
  • Staff-signal: restructures when the decision gets made, applies the same discipline to killing their own work, and has changed the vocabulary an organization reasons in.
  • Say this: "If no plausible result changes a decision, I don't run the study."

EQ05 — Safety-critical work ★ your closest analogue to this job

Asked by: clinical engineers, HF peers, regulatory · Target: ~90s · Structure: A

Base: "Tell me about work where the consequences were serious." / "What's your experience in safety-critical domains?" / "Have you done use-error analysis?"

Lead with this whenever you can. It is the only thing on your résumé that is medical, safety-critical, and surgical-adjacent at the same time.

Model answer (~230 words / 92s)

Situation. "At NASA Langley I led the human factors design validation for the next-generation medical workstations on the Lunar Gateway — where a crew member would actually be treated, with no evacuation and no second clinician. I was the PhD intern on that team, and this was my piece of it.

Task. We were evaluating operational environments and surgical-like workspace layouts in simulated microgravity, and my charge was to find the use errors that would actually hurt someone before the layouts were fixed.

Action. The method was a usability Failure Modes and Effects Analysis. I decomposed the clinical tasks, then went step by step asking how each one could fail — omission, wrong sequence, wrong object, wrong timing — and rated each by the severity of the harm it would produce rather than how often I thought it would happen. That surfaced the high-consequence input errors and the cognitive load bottlenecks. Then I redesigned the workstation layouts and the information displays against NASA-STD-3001 and MIL-STD-1472, off simulated-use testing data.

Result. Operator task completion time came down about thirty percent, and the critical system input errors were eliminated.

Earned secret. That was a use-related risk analysis before I had the regulatory vocabulary for it — uFMEA and the hazard-related use scenario chain in 62366 are the same reasoning with different paperwork.

Mechanism. And the ordering wasn't a preference, it was the risk control hierarchy: design it out, protect against it, tell people about it last — because a label is the weakest control there is."

Follow-ups

F1 — "How did you decide which tasks were critical?" (~70 words)

"By severity of harm, not probability. That's the part people get backwards. You can't estimate the probability of a use error reliably, which is exactly why the framework prioritizes on severity. So the question is never 'how likely is this' but 'what happens to the patient if it does' — and anything with an irreversible outcome goes on the list regardless of how rare it looks."

F2 — "Zero critical errors sounds like a small sample." (~60 words)

"It was, and I wouldn't present it as a validation result — it's a formative finding on a limited sample. What it justifies is a design decision, not a safety claim. The honest statement is that the redesign eliminated a specific error mode we'd observed repeatedly, not that the interface is error-free."

F3 — "What would you have done with more time?" (~60 words)

"Tested against a fatigued, task-loaded operator rather than a rested one. The layouts were evaluated under reasonable conditions, and the realistic condition for that workstation is someone who's been awake too long and is frightened. That's the gap I'd close, and I'd expect some of the error reduction to shrink under it."

Senior → Staff-signal

  • Senior: describes safety-critical work accurately with honest attribution.
  • Staff-signal: names the method and the standards, explains the severity-over-probability logic, and volunteers the limit of a clean result unprompted.
  • Say this: "I changed the layout rather than adding a warning. A label is the weakest control there is."

EQ06 — When you got it wrong

Asked by: HF peers, senior HF · Target: ~85s · Structure: A

Base: "Tell me about a study that didn't go as planned." / "Tell me about a failure." / "When has your research been wrong?"

⚠️ This one uses the master's thesis — it's a genuinely strong failure story and it's true, so it's worth the thirty seconds of graduate-work exposure. Swap in a professional example if you have one you'd rather tell.

Model answer (~215 words / 86s)

Situation. "My master's thesis was supposed to measure how long it takes to get back to a laparoscopic task after an interruption.

Task. The setup was the standard laparoscopic peg-transfer exercise, and I interrupted people the way the literature says surgeons actually get interrupted — somebody talks to you about something unrelated.

Action. Forty-six of my fifty-one participants never stopped. They kept moving pegs while they answered me. So they were distracted, not interrupted, and the dependent variable the whole study was built around stopped existing. A six-person pilot had shown me the opposite, which is exactly why I'd trusted it. I couldn't rescue the resumption measure, so I reported the null plainly rather than reframing the hypothesis around whatever turned out significant, and analyzed what the data could actually support — which was a clean task-demand effect on sequence errors.

Result. What it could support was that task demand drives errors. What it could not support was anything about resumption time, and I said so in the document and in the paper.

Earned secret. The failure mode I watch for now is confirmation at small n. A pilot that agrees with you is the most dangerous result you can get, because it buys confidence without buying evidence — the sample is too small to disagree.

Mechanism. So I pilot the manipulation check as its own question, separately from the effect. That's the design the dissertation used, and it's part of why I now get stakeholders to pre-commit to what each result changes."

Follow-ups

F1 — "How did you communicate it?" (~55 words)

"Early, and before the analysis. I brought the problem to stakeholders as soon as I could characterize it, so the conversation was about what the study could still answer rather than about whether I'd failed. Findings that arrive as a surprise cost you far more credibility than findings that arrive as bad news."

F2 — "How does that apply to validation testing here?" (~70 words)

"The same failure mode is live in any simulated-use study: you assume the scenario induced the condition it was designed to induce. If your use scenario is meant to create time pressure and participants don't actually feel it, your use errors aren't telling you what you think. So I'd treat the scenario itself as something to validate — with a check that doesn't depend on asking people whether they felt rushed."

F3 — "What do you do differently at design time now?" (~60 words)

"I write down what would have to be true for the study to work, and I test those assumptions first and separately. Most failed studies aren't analysis failures, they're assumption failures — and the assumption is usually that the manipulation did what the protocol said it would."

Senior → Staff-signal

  • Senior: tells a real failure honestly and says what they learned.
  • Staff-signal: identifies the class of error, and leaves behind a practice that prevents it recurring for other people.
  • Say this: "A pilot that confirms you is the most dangerous result you can get."

EQ07 — Working with clinicians

Asked by: clinical engineers, hiring manager · Target: ~80s · Structure: A

Base: "What's your experience working with surgeons?" / "How do you build credibility with clinical experts?" / "You'd be representing us with surgeons internationally."

They're screening for clinical humility. Overclaiming is the fatal move with this audience.

Model answer (~210 words / 84s)

Situation. "My clinical grounding comes from the Clinical Neuroscience Laboratory at Brigham and Women's, at Harvard Medical School.

Task. I coordinated clinical research protocols and led data collection for large-scale functional neuroimaging and physiological stress studies — fMRI, cardiovagal and heart-rate-variability telemetry, cortisol response curves, and behavioral performance under acute stress challenges, on longitudinal data for large patient cohorts.

Action. Two things came out of that. The first is procedural discipline: IRB protocols, HIPAA safety requirements, and federal clinical research standards, on data where getting it wrong has real consequences for real patients. Earned secret. My job in an operating room is to be the least disruptive person in it and still leave with the observation nobody wrote down.

⚠️ Your graduate studies used undergraduate participants, not clinicians. If it comes up, say so immediately and plainly. Never let an interviewer discover it.

Follow-ups

F1 — "Have you run studies with surgeons as participants?" (~55 words)

"Not as participants. My graduate work used novices by design, because it was isolating a cognitive mechanism rather than measuring expert performance. My clinical experience is protocol and data work at Brigham and clinical SME collaboration at NASA — real, but not surgeon-as-participant. That's a genuine gap, and it's part of what draws me here."

F2 — "A surgeon says your finding is wrong. What do you do?" (~70 words)

"Take it seriously, because on their procedure they're usually right and I'm usually missing context. Then separate two things: are they disagreeing with the observation, or with my interpretation of it? Those have different fixes. If it's the observation, I go look again. If it's the interpretation, that's the conversation I wanted them in the room for."

F3 — "How would you get surgeon time for generative work?" (~60 words)

"Make it cheap for them and valuable to them. Short sessions anchored to a specific remembered case rather than open-ended interviews, scheduled around their day instead of mine, and I come back with what changed because of what they said. The second session is much easier to get if the first one visibly went somewhere."

Senior → Staff-signal

  • Senior: demonstrates comfort in clinical settings and appropriate humility.
  • Staff-signal: volunteers the gap in their own clinical sampling before being asked, and describes a repeatable way of earning expert access.
  • Say this: "My job in an operating room is to be the least disruptive person in it and still leave with the observation nobody wrote down."

EQ08 — Being a design partner, not a design grader

Asked by: industrial and interaction designers, engineers · Target: ~80s · Structure: A

Base: "How do you work with designers?" / "Tell me about a cross-functional collaboration." / "How do you fit into the development process?"

The designers on this loop are deciding whether you'd make their work better or slower. They have met human factors people who show up at the end with a list.

Model answer (~205 words / 82s)

Situation. "At EchoStar I report to the VP of Product and lead human factors across software, physical hardware, and generative AI integrations. There was no function before me, which meant every bit of influence had to be earned rather than assigned.

Task. My charge was to make human factors something teams wanted early rather than something that happened to them late.

Action. The move that worked was bringing constraints instead of verdicts. Rather than reviewing a design and listing problems, I'd arrive at concept stage with the envelope — reach envelope and anatomical safety limits for physical hardware, perceptual thresholds for latency and feedback, criteria for screen layout and conversational interfaces. Designers can work inside a constraint; they can't do much with a critique of something already built. On one hardware program I brought the reach and anatomical safety envelope into the concept review rather than the design review, and a control placement moved before any tooling existed — which is a cheap change at that stage and an expensive one three months later. I also made a rule of never giving a finding without a candidate direction, even a bad one, because a finding with no path forward puts the whole cost of the problem on the designer.

Result. The hardware teams started pulling me into concept reviews by default rather than design reviews, which is the only durable version of this.

Earned secret. Human factors gets treated as a gate when it arrives late and as a partner when it arrives early — and that's almost entirely within my control, not theirs.

Mechanism. What I left behind was the org-wide usability criteria those teams now design against."

Follow-ups

F1 — "What if a designer pushes back on your constraint?" (~60 words)

"Then I need to be clear about which kind of constraint it is. Some are perceptual or anatomical facts and aren't negotiable. Others are my judgment about a tradeoff, and those genuinely are. Being explicit about which is which is what keeps me credible — if I defend everything equally hard, nothing I say carries weight."

F2 — "How do you work with engineers versus designers?" (~65 words)

"Different currency. Designers need the constraint early and the freedom inside it. Engineers need a number with a tolerance and a defined behavior when it can't be met. Same underlying finding, two different artifacts — and the failure mode is handing an engineer a design principle or handing a designer an acceptance criterion."

F3 — "What would your first ninety days look like?" (~70 words)

"Read the existing record first — known use problems, complaint data, prior risk analyses — because that's the cheapest evidence available and it's already a required input. Then get into the environment: observation, and time with the team roles I'd be designing for. I'd want to be useful on something small and real early rather than arriving with a plan for how everyone should work."

Senior → Staff-signal

  • Senior: collaborates well and communicates findings clearly.
  • Staff-signal: frames the partner-versus-gate dynamic as their own responsibility, and distinguishes non-negotiable constraints from judgment calls out loud.
  • Say this: "Designers can work inside a constraint. They can't do much with a critique of something already built."

MQ01 — The haptics question ★ expect a version of this

Asked by: hiring manager, senior HF, engineers · Target: ~3 min · Structure: B, extended

Base: "How would you approach research for force feedback on a surgical system?" / any "how would you research X" hypothetical.

Announce the sequence first: "Let me take that in order. What already exists, what's already known, what I'd ask experts, what I'd test once there's something to test, and what it all has to turn into."

Model answer (~380 words / 3 min — deliberately long)

The claim. "The first question isn't whether surgeons want force feedback. It's what their current visual compensation strategy is doing for them, and what restoring force changes about it.

What already exists. Three reference points bound the problem. Open surgery, where the surgeon feels tissue directly and their whole model is built on that. Laparoscopic, where force arrives through a long instrument and a pivot — attenuated and distorted, and surgeons have adapted to that degraded signal for decades. And current robotic practice, where surgeons compensate visually: they read how tissue deforms instead of feeling resistance.

What's already known. Before generating anything I'd go through complaint data and known use problems on the fielded system, plus the literature. Known use problems are free evidence and the cheapest hypotheses I'll get — and they're a required input to the process anyway.

What I'd ask experts. Cognitive task analysis with expert surgeons about how they work now — not 'would you like force feedback,' but what cues are you using, when would force information have changed a decision, where do you feel blind. Anchored in specific remembered cases rather than generalities, because that's where the real reasoning surfaces. Contextual inquiry in the OR. And workflow mapping across the whole team — the bedside assistant and the scrub are users too.

What I'd test. Once there's something to react to: cognitive walkthroughs, then formative testing at increasing fidelity, each round scoped to a decision agreed in advance.

The decision it drives. The output isn't a report. It's verifiable design inputs — a detectable force difference, a latency bound, a defined behavior when feedback drops out — plus the first entries in the use-related risk analysis. Those early entries are what the submission gets argued from years later.

What would change my mind. If cognitive task analysis shows the visual compensation strategy is better than force in the cases that matter — more accurate, less effortful — then force feedback is solving a problem surgeons already solved, and the work shifts to not disrupting it."

⚠️ One precision point. Cognitive task analysis studies how experts think in current practice, needs no prototype, and generates. Cognitive walkthroughs and formative testing evaluate a design, need something to look at, and evaluate. Using them interchangeably tells a specialist you learned the vocabulary rather than the practice.

Follow-ups

F1 — "What are the risks of adding force feedback?" (~90 words)

"Six, and the one people miss is negative transfer. Experienced robotic surgeons have trained the visual compensation strategy — new force cues may interfere with a skill they've built. I'd measure that as an expert-versus-novice gap and expect experts to do worse initially. The others: visual and haptic disagreeing, which is worse than one cue alone; latency, where force arriving late stops feeling like it came from the instrument; over-trust, where visual cross-checking drops off; habituation over a long case; and losing feedback mid-case, which is an unannounced mode change."

F2 — "Why not just put a force number on the screen?" (~85 words)

"Different workload profiles, not different quality levels. A visual indicator is cheaper, easier to verify, and degrades gracefully — but it spends the visual channel, already the bottleneck in the operative field, and costs an attention shift. True haptics uses an unloaded channel and needs no attention shift, but brings latency, calibration, and the risk of disagreeing with the eyes. Which is right depends on where the surgeon's spare capacity actually is during that specific critical task — and that's empirical, before the architecture gets chosen."

F3 — "Six weeks, no prototype. What do you do?" (~75 words)

"Everything upstream of the prototype, which is most of the value anyway. Complaint data and known use problems in week one. Cognitive task analysis with eight to twelve expert surgeons through weeks two to four, anchored on remembered cases. Workflow mapping across roles. Then I'd deliver the draft use-related risk analysis and a first set of design inputs — so when a prototype exists the evaluation is already scoped and the risk file isn't starting from zero."

MQ02 — From observation to design input ★ the most valuable thing you can say

Asked by: everyone · Target: ~90s · Structure: B

Base: "How do you turn research into requirements?" / "What does your output look like?" / "How do you author usability design inputs?"

This is exactly what the job description means by authoring usability design inputs. Say the chain end to end.

Model answer (~225 words / 90s)

The claim. "My output isn't a report, it's a design input an engineer can verify without me in the room. Let me walk the chain — and I'll make the numbers up, because the point is the shape.

The chain. Observation: surgeons tell me they're uncertain about grip on friable tissue — vessel wall, bowel — and compensate by watching how it deforms. Need: the user has to distinguish a safe grip from an unsafe one without taking their eyes off the operative field. Design input: the system shall present force information such that a one-newton difference is discriminable within a hundred and fifty milliseconds of contact, without requiring a visual fixation shift.

And the risk analysis entry, in the same breath. Hazard: mechanical force. Sequence of events: the surgeon can't perceive grip force, so grip escalates. Hazardous situation: a vessel or bowel wall exposed to force beyond what it tolerates. Harm: laceration or bleeding. Critical task: grip application during grasping and retraction. Candidate risk control: the force display, with effectiveness verified in formative evaluation before the first integrated prototype review.

The decision it drives. That's the whole path — from something somebody said to something an engineer can test against, and to a traceable entry somebody can find three years later.

What would change my mind. If the discriminable difference turns out far outside what the hardware can deliver, the design input was written against the wrong control, and I'd go back to the need rather than relax the number."

⚠️ Say "I'll make the numbers up" out loud. It lets you be fully concrete without claiming to know their thresholds — and being concrete is the entire value of this answer.

F1 — "Who owns that requirement once you've written it?" (~60 words)

F2 — "What if engineering says that's not feasible?" (~65 words)

"Then we substitute a channel rather than relaxing the number, and the need stays fixed. MQ03 — Psychophysics into a specification ★ your deepest technical story

Asked by: engineers, senior HF, hiring manager · Target: ~90s · Structure: B

Base: "Tell me about the latency work." / "How do you set a threshold?" / "How do you make a perceptual finding actionable?"

The strongest methodological evidence you own — a multi-year program with a real design, a counterintuitive finding, and a specification at the end.

Model answer (~230 words / 92s)

The claim. "A latency target is a claim about human perception, so it should be measured like one and written like one — not set as a round number somebody liked.

How I'd know. At Amazon I ran a multi-year psychophysics program to replace Alexa's arbitrary engineering latency targets. Within-subjects, thirty participants, twelve high-priority interaction types crossed with six latency levels from five hundred milliseconds to three seconds — roughly two thousand trials. Wizard-of-Oz instrumentation gave me per-trial millisecond control of the response delay. And I used a three-point slowness scale rather than a Likert agreement scale, because people perceive slowness, not speed — asking in the direction users actually experience it is what made the ratings usable.

Evidence, and where it stops. Two findings mattered more than the numbers. Acceptable latency is intent-dependent, not global — interactions that mimic a real-world action need roughly five hundred milliseconds, others tolerate far more. And the response curve is non-monotonic, so conversational responses need a floor as well as a ceiling: too fast reads as canned, not as fast. The boundary is real though — thresholds are specific to the channel and the workload. A consumer visual-feedback threshold does not port to a gloved, sterile, high-tempo console. The method ports. The number doesn't.

The decision it drives. The output was a two-tier threshold specification per interaction type with defined pass criteria — an acceptance test, not a report. Alexa Economics applied it to their Negative Value Action model and projected about fifty million dollars in incremental operational value over fifteen months.

What would change my mind. If users under realistic workload show no performance or trust difference across the threshold band, latency isn't the binding constraint and I'd re-derive it under load."

⚠️ $50M is the only dollar figure you ever say, and always with its attribution — the economic model was Alexa Economics' work; your contribution is the threshold structure. Never allude to anything larger.

Follow-ups

F1 — "Why a three-point scale and not a seven-point Likert?" (~65 words)

"Because the construct is slowness, and users don't experience speed as a symmetric agreement scale. A coarse scale in the direction people actually perceive gives cleaner discrimination than a fine-grained scale measuring the wrong thing. It also reduces per-trial cost, which matters across roughly two thousand trials where you need participant attention to hold."

F2 — "Non-monotonic is counterintuitive. Explain." (~70 words)

"For conversational responses, a reply that arrives too fast reads as canned — it signals the system didn't actually process anything. So the perception curve isn't 'faster is always better,' it has an optimum. That's why the spec needed a lower bound as well as an upper one, and it's the clearest example I have of why you measure a perceptual target instead of assuming its shape."

F3 — "How does that transfer to a surgical console?" (~70 words)

"The method transfers completely and the numbers not at all. Same questions: what force difference can a surgeon actually discriminate, at what delay does force stop feeling like it originated at the instrument, what's the defined behavior when the hardware can't deliver. Each becomes a design input with a percentile and a fallback, and each becomes an entry in the risk analysis."

Senior → Staff-signal

  • Senior: reports a threshold and the study behind it.
  • Staff-signal: justifies a measurement-instrument choice on construct grounds, names the non-monotonic finding, and shows the population-to-specification translation.
  • Say this: "The durable output wasn't the number. It was that latency stopped being opinion — anyone proposing a target had to say which interaction type and which percentile."

MQ04 — Use-error analysis ★ the method this job runs on

Asked by: HF peers, regulatory, clinical engineers · Target: ~85s · Structure: B

Base: "Walk me through how you'd do a use-related risk analysis." / "How do you find use errors before testing?" / "What's your uFMEA experience?" / "How many participants?"

Model answer (~215 words / 86s)

The claim. "Use-error analysis is where the design gets decided, not where it gets checked. If it happens after the architecture is fixed, everything it finds is expensive.

How I'd know. I'd work top-down and bottom-up and see whether they agree. Top-down: start from harms — what can this device do to a patient — and work backward to the interactions that produce them. Bottom-up: a usability Failure Modes and Effects Analysis. Decompose the procedure with a hierarchical task analysis, then take each step and ask systematically how it fails — omission, commission, wrong sequence, wrong timing, wrong object, wrong amount. Anything reachable that leads to serious harm becomes a critical task. Where the two directions disagree is usually where the real problem is.

Evidence, and where it stops. I did exactly this at NASA Langley on the Lunar Gateway medical workstations — uFMEA across the clinical task set, then redesign against the failures it surfaced. The limit: that was a workstation with a small SME pool, not a fielded product with post-market data. Here you'd have complaint data and known use problems, which is better evidence than anything I had — and a required input.

The decision it drives. The output is the critical task list plus candidate risk controls, each tied to the use scenario it came from — so the traceability argument exists before anyone needs it. And it sets the sample logic downstream: formative work should be small and iterative, five participants three times rather than fifteen once, because the point is changing the design between rounds. Validation is a different animal — generally at least fifteen per distinct user group. Treating a formative study as a small validation is the most common way the record ends up weak.

What would change my mind. If formative testing repeatedly surfaces error modes the analysis never predicted, the task decomposition is wrong and I'd rebuild it from observation rather than from documentation."

Follow-ups

F1 — "How do you keep it from becoming a spreadsheet nobody reads?" (~65 words)

"By pruning on severity early. The failure mode is enumerating everything and weighting nothing, and you end up with a document that's complete and useless. I'd rather have twenty critical tasks everyone can name than four hundred rows nobody has read. The severity cut is what makes it a decision tool instead of an artifact."

F2 — "Where do known use problems come from?" (~60 words)

"Complaint data, service records, adverse event reporting, prior validation findings, and the published literature for the device class. They're a required input under 62366, but the practical reason to start there is that they're free and they're real — someone has already run the experiment on a fielded system at a scale I could never fund."

F3 — "How does that feed the risk file?" (~65 words)

"Human factors owns the use-related portion — use errors, hazard-related use scenarios, the critical task list, and the evidence that the risk controls work. That feeds the broader risk management file. The failure mode is those living separately, where a use-related control gets credited without anyone testing whether users perceive it."

Senior → Staff-signal

  • Senior: can describe a use-error analysis process.
  • Staff-signal: runs it from both directions and treats the disagreement as signal, and prunes on severity to keep it a decision tool.
  • Say this: "Where the top-down and bottom-up analyses disagree is usually where the real problem is."

MQ05 — Measuring workload and cognitive load

Asked by: HF peers, senior HF · Target: ~85s · Structure: B

Base: "How do you measure cognitive load?" / "When is a physiological measure worth it?" / "What do you think of NASA-TLX?"

Model answer (~215 words / 86s)

The claim. "Instrument choice follows the decision, not the prestige of the signal. Objective measures earn their cost in two cases: when you need within-task dynamics — where in the procedure load spikes — or when self-report is structurally compromised by the task itself.

How I'd know. I work down a ladder and stop at the cheapest rung that answers the question. Behavioral performance and system telemetry first — errors, mode confusions, time to acknowledge. Then eye tracking, when the question is attention allocation and specifically what someone never looked at, which no post-task rating recovers. Then continuous physiological measures when I need a time series across a long, uneven task. NASA-TLX stays in the kit as a cheap, comparable post-task summary — it just can't tell me when.

Evidence, and where it stops. At Amazon I co-established the company's first objective framework for measuring cognitive load in complex interfaces, using fNIRS neuroimaging to quantify neural activation patterns that identify cognitive friction. Separately, at Brigham I processed fMRI, cardiovagal telemetry, and cortisol response curves for acute-stress research — two different toolkits in two different settings, and I keep them separate. And to be precise: I don't have a head-to-head result showing a biometric measure outperformed NASA-TLX. My claim is about which class of question each answers.

The decision it drives. A biometric measure gets funded only when it's pre-committed to a decision. If no plausible result changes anything, it's expensive theater and I'd kill it myself.

What would change my mind. If the physiological signal never dissociates from behavioral performance across conditions, it's redundant and I drop it."

⚠️ fNIRS belongs to Amazon. fMRI, ECG, and cortisol belong to Brigham. Never merge them, and never claim a bake-off win over NASA-TLX.

Follow-ups

F1 — "Take fNIRS away. What's next best, and what can you no longer claim?" (~65 words)

"Eye tracking plus task-embedded performance probes. What I lose is the claim about internal load when behavior looks fine — the compensating-operator case, where performance holds while effort climbs and the only visible signal is the collapse later. Without a continuous measure I can infer that from degradation over time, but I can't assert it in the moment."

F2 — "How do you know it's load and not just arousal?" (~70 words)

"Dissociation, and it's the right challenge — arousal confounds are the standard failure of this method class. The design requirement is conditions matched on arousal but differing in the cognitive demand you care about. If the signal moves with demand and not with arousal, you have something. If it moves with both, you have a stress detector, which is a much weaker claim and I'd report it as one."

F3 — "Would any of that work in an OR?" (~65 words)

"Optical and cardiac signals suffer motion artifact, and eye tracking suffers lighting and recalibration — so I'd assume degradation and design the field version around it. In the operating room I'd lean on observation and system telemetry: dwell, mode errors, corrections, sequence breakdowns. The physiological work stays in the simulator where I can control it. The field's job is confirming the effect exists in reality, not measuring it precisely."

Senior → Staff-signal

  • Senior: selects a valid instrument and runs it well.
  • Staff-signal: names the decision the measure serves, offers the cheaper substitute unprompted, and states the limit of their own evidence without being pushed.
  • Say this: "I've made a career out of objective measurement, which is exactly why I'll tell you when not to buy it."

MQ07 — Multimodal interaction and mode confusion ★ where your patent earns its place

Asked by: senior HF, engineers, designers · Target: ~85s · Structure: B

Base: "How do you think about multimodal interfaces?" / "How would you prevent mode confusion?" / "Tell me about your patent."

Mode confusion is a known use-error theme on surgical robots — which arm, which control, what's active. Your patent is literally about context-dependent control mapping.

Model answer (~210 words / 84s)

The claim. "Mode errors aren't user carelessness. They're a mismatch between what the system thinks is active and what the user believes is active — and that's a design property you can specify against.

How I'd know. My patent is on context-based control inputs: the same physical actuation resolving to a different command depending on the interface presented and the user's position within it. Building that meant spending real time on the failure mode it creates — the moment the user's model of what's active diverges from the truth. The design rules that fall out are concrete. State has to be perceptible without a deliberate check, because a user under load won't make one. Transitions between modes need to be announced in a channel that isn't already saturated. And the more consequential the action, the less the system should rely on the user having tracked state correctly.

Evidence, and where it stops. That was consumer multimodal hardware — visual, haptic, auditory, conversational — across the Echo portfolio, and the patent is public so it's checkable. The limit is that a mode error there costs someone an annoyance, not a patient. The mechanism transfers; the tolerance for it doesn't.

The decision it drives. In a surgical context I'd expect that to become a critical task determination: any control transition where the wrong mode produces irreversible harm gets designed so state is continuously perceptible, not recoverable by inference.

What would change my mind. If observation shows experienced users track mode reliably through cues the interface doesn't explicitly provide, then the answer is protecting those cues rather than adding new indicators."

Follow-ups

"No, and that's the weak version. An indicator relies on the user choosing to look, which is exactly what fails under load. F2 — "How would you measure mode confusion?" (~65 words)

"Behaviorally, not by asking. "It means one specific architecture is documented under my name — it's a public document, so it's checkable. What I'd bring here is the reasoning behind it rather than the claim itself: how context-dependence buys you interface density, and what it costs you in state ambiguity."

RQ03 — The risk framework, and what didn't change

Asked by: regulatory, engineers, senior HF · Target: ~85s · Structure: B

Base: "How do 62366 and 14971 fit together?" / "Walk me through use-related risk analysis." / "What's your risk analysis experience?"

Anyone can recite what's new. Knowing what held is the tell that you understand the framework.

Model answer (~210 words / 84s)

The claim. "The load-bearing logic didn't change: critical tasks are prioritized by severity of potential harm, not by probability — because the probability of a use error can't be estimated reliably. That one sentence holds up the entire framework.

The process chain, from 62366. Use specification, then user interface characteristics related to safety, then known use problems, then hazard-related use scenarios, then critical tasks, then the evaluation plan, then formative evaluation, then validation. The chain is the traceability argument — every critical task has to have come from somewhere, and every risk control has to get evaluated.

The causal chain, from 14971. Hazard, then sequence of events, then hazardous situation, then harm — in that order. Most people collapse hazard and hazardous situation; not collapsing them is an instant credibility marker. And the control hierarchy is design it out, protect against it, tell people about it.

The decision it drives. Validation expectations held too: generally at least fifteen participants per distinct user group, representative US users, production-equivalent interface with final labeling, and no closing out an observed use error with labeling or training alone without evidence the fix works.

Why it matters for my actual job. The submission category and the validation argument get settled years downstream — but they're settled by the risk analysis somebody created at the very beginning. So I treat early generative work as the first draft of the submission argument."

Follow-ups

F1 — "Give me a concrete hazard chain." (~70 words)

"Hazard: mechanical force. Sequence of events: the surgeon can't perceive grip force, so grip escalates. Hazardous situation: a vessel or bowel wall exposed to force beyond what it tolerates. Harm: laceration or bleeding. Critical task: grip application during grasping and retraction. Candidate risk control: the force display — with effectiveness verified in formative evaluation, not assumed."

F2 — "What makes a risk analysis weak?" (~60 words)

"Critical tasks that appear without traceable derivation, and risk controls credited without evaluation. If I can't walk backwards from a critical task to the use scenario and the known use problem that produced it, the chain is broken — and that's the expensive thing to fix three years later, when the argument has to be made from the record."

F3 — "How do you handle a risk control that's also a use error source?" (~65 words)

"You analyze it like any other interface element, because it is one. A confirmation step added to prevent one error creates its own failure modes — habituation, and the error of confirming reflexively. So a risk control isn't finished when it's specified; it's finished when it's been evaluated for the errors it introduces as well as the ones it prevents."
