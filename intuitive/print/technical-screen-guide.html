SQ03 — Defining a workflow ★ named directly in the track description

Asked by: either · Target: ~85s · Structure: B

Base: "How do you define a user workflow?" / "How would you map a procedure?" / "What does workflow definition actually produce?"

User workflow definition is one of the four things Reema listed for this track. Have a real answer, not a gesture at task analysis.

Model answer (~210 words / 85s)

The claim. "A workflow definition is a design artifact, not documentation. If it only describes what people do today, it can't tell you what a new system has to support — so I build it to be argued with.

How I'd know. It starts from observation and cognitive task analysis, not from a document. Then a hierarchical decomposition, taken to the level where a single step can fail. Three things get annotated on top of the steps, and they're where the value is. Who holds what state at each moment. Where control or attention hands off between roles. And where the path goes off-nominal — because the normal case is the easy half, and the interesting failures live in recovery.

Evidence, and where it stops. I've built these for multi-role systems: the clinical task set on the NASA workstation, and across the Echo portfolio where several people share one device with different intents. The limit is real — I haven't mapped a full surgical procedure with a sterile field and a five-person team. I'd expect the synchronization points and the off-nominals to be where my first draft is wrong, which is why I'd walk it with the bedside staff and not just the console surgeon.

The decision it drives. The workflow is what you write design inputs against. It tells you which steps are critical tasks, which transitions need state to be perceptible, and where a new capability has to fit without displacing something already load-bearing.

What would change my mind. If observation shows the team routinely runs a path my decomposition doesn't contain, the map is wrong. I rebuild it from observation rather than patch it."

Follow-ups

F1 — "How do you handle variation between surgeons or sites?" (~65 words)

"Map the variation rather than averaging it away, because the average workflow is one nobody runs. I'd look for what's invariant — the steps that must happen in that order for safety reasons — and treat everything else as a range the design has to tolerate. Where a site's variant creates a hazard the others don't, that's a finding, not noise."

F2 — "What's the actual deliverable?" (~60 words)

"A decomposition somebody can disagree with specifically: steps, roles, state, handoffs, off-nominals. It feeds three things — the critical task list, the design inputs, and the scenarios a formative study is built from. If it isn't generating those, it's a diagram, and a diagram nobody makes a decision from is decoration."

The Staff-signal

  • Staff-signal: annotates state, handoffs, and off-nominals; treats variation as signal; names what the workflow is for downstream.
  • Say this: "The normal case is the easy half. The interesting failures live in recovery."

MQ02 — From observation to design input ★ the most valuable thing you say all hour

Asked by: Valerie, or either · Target: ~90s · Structure: B

Base: "How do you turn what surgeons tell you into something engineers can build against?" / "How do you author interface design requirements?" / "What does your output look like?"

Interface design requirements is the second thing Reema named. Say the chain end to end, with numbers — being concrete is the entire value of this answer.

Model answer (~225 words / 90s)

The claim. "My output isn't a report, it's a design input an engineer can verify without me in the room. Take a hypothetical new grasper for friable tissue — I'll make the numbers up, because the point is the shape.

The chain. Observation: surgeons tell me they're uncertain about grip on friable tissue — vessel wall, bowel — and compensate by watching how it deforms. Need: the user has to distinguish a safe grip from an unsafe one at the moment of application, not after the tissue responds. Design input: the system shall present force information such that a one-newton difference is discriminable within a hundred and fifty milliseconds of contact.

And the risk analysis entry, in the same breath. Hazard: mechanical force. Sequence of events: the surgeon can't perceive grip force, so grip escalates. Hazardous situation: a vessel or bowel wall exposed to force beyond what it tolerates. Harm: laceration or bleeding. Critical task: grip application during grasping and retraction. Candidate risk control: the force display, with effectiveness verified in formative evaluation before the first integrated prototype review.

The decision it drives. That's the whole path — from something somebody said, to something an engineer can test against, to a traceable entry somebody can find three years later.

What would change my mind. If the discriminable difference turns out far outside what the hardware can deliver, the design input was written against the wrong control, and I'd go back to the need rather than relax the number."

Follow-ups

F1 — "What if engineering says that's not feasible?" (~65 words)

"Then we substitute a channel rather than relaxing the number, and the need stays fixed. If the haptic path can't deliver discriminability in time, the question becomes which other channel carries the same information at acceptable cost. What degrades is completeness, not safety — and if nothing can carry it, that's a documented residual risk with a name against it, not an omission."

F2 — "Who owns that requirement once you've written it?" (~55 words)

"Human factors owns the claim about the user; systems owns whether it's achievable. It's a joint artifact, and it isn't done until an engineer agrees it's verifiable. A design input nobody can test against isn't a requirement — it's a recommendation with confident formatting."

F3 — "Where does the number itself come from?" (~60 words)

"Psychophysics, with the uncertainty carried forward — an adaptive procedure converging on the detection point, and the spec takes the conservative bound of the interval rather than the mean. And you choose the population percentile deliberately and write down who it excludes, because a central-tendency spec silently designs for the median user and fails the tails."

SQ04 — Prototyping and fidelity ★ early prototype development, in one story

Asked by: either · Target: ~80s · Structure: B

Base: "How do you decide what to prototype?" / "What fidelity do you build?" / "Tell me about a rig you built."

Early prototype development is the fourth thing Reema named, and this is the one answer where you get to sound like someone who builds things rather than studies them.

Model answer (~205 words / 82s)

The claim. "Fidelity is a decision variable, not a quality level. You build the cheapest thing that can produce the specific answer you need, and deliberately leave everything else rough.

How I'd know — the clearest example I have. On the Alexa latency program I needed per-trial millisecond control of response delay across twelve interaction types. A real system couldn't give me that: its latency was a distribution, not a setting. So I built a Wizard-of-Oz rig — the interaction looked and sounded production-real to the participant, but the response timing was mine, trial by trial. That's deliberately lopsided fidelity: exact where the question lived, near-zero effort everywhere else.

The decision it served. It turned latency from something you observe into something you manipulate. That's the whole reason the output could be a threshold specification with pass criteria rather than a benchmark of how the current system felt.

Evidence, and where it stops. That was consumer hardware, and a Wizard-of-Oz setup only works while the participant can't detect the seam. For a surgical task with force and tissue response the seam is much harder to hide — I'd expect to need a real instrument in the loop far earlier, and I'd treat 'can they tell' as something to check rather than assume.

What would change my mind. If participants behave differently when they suspect a person is in the loop, the rig is measuring compliance rather than perception, and it has to be replaced with an instrumented real system."

Follow-ups

F1 — "When would you build higher fidelity than the question needs?" (~60 words)

"When the question is about the physical thing itself — reach, grip, weight, how it behaves in the hand, what it's like gowned and gloved. You can't fake proprioception with a foam mock-up. Anything about perception of the object gets real material and real geometry; anything about information and sequence can stay cheap much longer."

F2 — "What's the earliest thing you'd put in front of a surgeon?" (~60 words)

"Something that makes disagreement cheap — a walkthrough of the workflow, not a UI. Storyboard the procedure step by step and ask where it's wrong. You learn whether you've understood the task before you've spent anything on an interface, and a surgeon will correct a wrong sequence far faster than they'll critique a screen."

The Staff-signal

  • Staff-signal: treats fidelity as lopsided on purpose, names the decision the rig served, and flags the condition under which the method breaks.
  • Say this: "Exact where the question lives, near-zero effort everywhere else."

MQ01 — The research hypothetical ★ and the fact that force feedback already ships

Asked by: either · Target: ~3 min · Structure: B, extended

Base: "How would you approach research for force feedback on a surgical system?" / any "how would you research X" hypothetical.

⚠️ Force feedback is not hypothetical, and they built it. It shipped on da Vinci 5 in the US in March 2024 — tip sensors relaying push and pull forces to the console hand controllers — and the Force Gauge, a visual indicator of tip force from 0 to 6.5 newtons, followed in September 2025. Answering this as a greenfield problem tells two Intuitive human factors engineers you don't know their flagship.

Announce the sequence after the claim: "Let me take that in order. What already exists, what's already known, what I'd ask experts, what I'd test, and what it all has to turn into."

Model answer (~355 words / 3 min — deliberately long)

The claim. "Force feedback is already fielded on da Vinci 5, pairing haptic cues with a visual gauge — so the research question isn't whether force information helps. It's where each channel earns its place: which tasks and procedures, what happens when haptic and visual disagree, and how experienced visual-compensation users adapt. I'll make that concrete for extending it to a new instrument or procedure.

What already exists. Three reference points bound it. Open surgery, where the surgeon feels tissue directly. Laparoscopic, where force arrives through a long instrument and a pivot, attenuated and distorted. And robotic practice before force feedback, where surgeons compensated visually. That third group is the interesting one, because they trained a strategy the hardware now partly replaces.

What's already known. Complaint data and known use problems on the fielded system, plus whatever the post-market record says about how the capability is actually being used. Free evidence and the cheapest hypotheses I'll get.

What I'd ask experts. Cognitive task analysis with expert surgeons about how they work now — which cues they're actually using, where the gauge earns a glance and where the haptic channel carries it alone, when force information changed a decision, where they still feel blind. Anchored in specific remembered cases. And workflow mapping across the whole team, because the bedside assistant and the scrub are users too.

What I'd test. Once there's something to react to: cognitive walkthroughs, then formative testing at increasing fidelity, each round scoped to a decision agreed in advance.

The decision it drives. The output isn't a report. It's verifiable design inputs — a detectable force difference, a latency bound, a defined behavior when feedback drops out — plus entries in the use-related risk analysis. Those entries are what the submission gets argued from years later.

What would change my mind. If cognitive task analysis shows experienced users are still running the visual compensation strategy and largely ignoring the force channel, the work isn't tuning the signal. It's understanding why a shipped capability isn't being adopted, which is a different study entirely."

Follow-ups

F1 — "What are the risks of adding force feedback?" (~90 words)

"Six, and on a fielded capability they get sharper. The one people miss is negative transfer: experienced robotic surgeons trained the visual compensation strategy, so force cues can interfere with a skill they've built. I'd measure that as an expert-versus-novice gap and expect experts to do worse initially. The others: haptic and visual disagreeing, which is worse than one cue alone; latency, where force arriving late stops feeling like it came from the instrument; over-trust, where visual cross-checking decays; habituation; and losing feedback mid-case, which is an unannounced mode change."

F2 — "Six weeks, no prototype. What do you do?" (~75 words)

"Everything upstream of the prototype, which is most of the value anyway. Complaint data and known use problems in week one. Cognitive task analysis with eight to twelve expert surgeons through weeks two to four, anchored on remembered cases. Workflow mapping across roles. Then I'd deliver the draft use-related risk analysis and a first set of design inputs — so when a prototype exists the evaluation is already scoped and the risk file isn't starting from zero."

⚠️ One precision point, and both of them will hear it. Cognitive task analysis studies how experts think in current practice, needs no prototype, and generates. Cognitive walkthroughs and formative testing need something to look at, and evaluate. Using them interchangeably tells a specialist you learned the vocabulary rather than the practice.

The Staff-signal

  • Staff-signal: separates generative from evaluative methods precisely, names the counterintuitive risk, and ties early work to the regulatory record it becomes.
  • Say this: "I treat generative work as the first draft of the submission argument, not as pre-work."

If they hand you a scenario or a device

Same six steps as MQ04, said out loud before you start — naming the method is half the signal.

  1. Users, uses, environment. Who touches it, which procedure phase, sterile or non-sterile.
  2. Task decomposition. Steps at a level where each one can fail.
  3. Failure modes per step. Omission, wrong sequence, wrong object, wrong timing.
  4. Severity triage. Anything that can irreversibly harm the patient is a critical task.
  5. Controls and design inputs. Design it out, protect against it, inform last — each a verifiable shall.
  6. Evaluation. What formative test would show the control works.

⚠️ Narrate as an analyst, not an enthusiast. Observe and ask; don't perform. If you don't know how something works, say so and ask — guessing at a mechanism in front of the people who built it is the one unrecoverable move.

MQ04 — Use-error analysis, and safety-critical work ★ your closest analogue

Asked by: Elyse · Target: ~90s · Structure: B

Model answer (~240 words / 95s)

The claim. "Use-error analysis is where the design gets decided, not where it gets checked. If it happens after the architecture is fixed, everything it finds is expensive.

How I'd know. I work top-down and bottom-up and see whether they agree. Top-down: start from harms — what can this device do to a patient — and work backward to the interactions that produce them. Bottom-up: a usability FMEA. Decompose the procedure with a hierarchical task analysis, then take each step and ask systematically how it fails — omission, commission, wrong sequence, wrong timing, wrong object, wrong amount. Critical tasks are decided by severity of harm, because use-error probability can't be estimated reliably. Probability still belongs in the overall risk estimate — I just don't let an optimistic probability take a severe scenario off the critical task list. Where the two directions disagree is usually where the real problem is.

Evidence, and where it stops. I did exactly this at NASA Langley on the Lunar Gateway medical workstations — where a crew member would be treated with no evacuation and no second clinician. uFMEA across the clinical task set. The highest-severity failure modes came from adjacent controls whose consequences differed if hit at the wrong time, so the redesign separated them over three to four iterations, against NASA-STD-3001 and MIL-STD-1472. No critical input errors were observed in the final round [CONFIRM: N], and task time came down about thirty percent as a side effect, not the target — on a limited sample, so that's a formative finding, not a validation result. And it was a workstation with a small SME pool, not a fielded product with post-market data. Here you'd have complaint data, which is better evidence than anything I had.

The decision it drives. The output is the critical task list plus controls, each tied to the scenario it came from. And the ordering isn't a preference, it's the risk control hierarchy — I changed the layout rather than adding a warning, because a label is the weakest control there is.

Earned secret. That was a use-related risk analysis before I had the regulatory vocabulary for it. The uFMEA and the hazard-related use scenario chain are the same reasoning with different paperwork."

Follow-ups

F1 — "How do you keep it from becoming a spreadsheet nobody reads?" (~60 words)

"By pruning on severity early. The failure mode is enumerating everything and weighting nothing, and you end up with a document that's complete and useless. I'd rather have twenty critical tasks everyone on the team can name than four hundred rows nobody has read. The severity cut is what makes it a decision tool instead of an artifact."

F2 — "How do you handle a risk control that's also a use-error source?" (~60 words)

"You analyze it like any other interface element, because it is one. A confirmation step added to prevent one error creates its own failure modes — habituation, and the error of confirming reflexively. So a risk control isn't finished when it's specified. It's finished when it's been evaluated for the errors it introduces as well as the ones it prevents."

F3 — "When is residual risk acceptable?" (~60 words)

"Acceptability isn't negotiated case by case. It's defined in the risk management plan before any hazard is analyzed. I drive each use-related risk down the control hierarchy — design it out, then protective measures, then information for safety — verify the controls work, and evaluate what remains against those criteria. Overall residual risk is weighed against clinical benefit. My job is making sure nothing is residual by omission."

⚠️ Friday you framed acceptance as a negotiation with program management, and "not my decision." Augustina corrected it: pre-established criteria, a living risk file, mitigation through design first. Never frame it as a negotiation again.
