Part 2 · How to build an answer on your feet

Two shapes. Pick the right one.

Almost every question is one of two kinds, and each wants a different shape. Using the wrong one costs you even when the content is right — telling a story in answer to a method question reads as dodging.

When they ask about something you did — tell a story.

Set the scene. Say what you were asked to do. Then what you did, with the hardest moment at the center. Then what happened.

Then the two beats most people skip:

  • The earned secret — the thing you know that most people in your job don't.
  • What you left behind — the standard, the gate, or the document that outlived you.

"What I left behind was X. And Y still has to clear it."

That last beat is the whole difference between "executed well" and "senior." Say it as its own sentence, out loud, every time.

Ninety seconds to about a hundred and ten.

When they ask what you think — reason out loud.

Lead with your answer in the first sentence; don't wind up. Then how you'd know. Then your evidence — and where that evidence stops, before anyone has to ask. Then the design input it produces. Then what would change your mind.

Sixty-five to ninety seconds.

Naming the limit of your own evidence, unprompted, buys more credibility than any extra proof point. It's also the clearest thing separating you from someone who's confident and wrong.

Timing

You speak at roughly 150 words a minute. If a ninety-second answer keeps running past two minutes, the cause is almost always a second example — not too much detail. Cut the example, keep the detail.

Follow-ups get twenty to thirty-five seconds. A follow-up is a scalpel; answer it with a scalpel. Answer the one thing asked, land it, stop. Re-telling the original story on a follow-up is the most common way strong candidates lose a round.

Story 1 · The interruption research ★ this is your opener

⚠️ Fix this before you say anything to anyone.

The laparoscopy study is your master's thesis. Your dissertation is the data-entry study. Your résumé and your earlier notes had them backwards. Both are published under your name in Old Dominion's public repository, and the people interviewing you have PhDs. Getting your own titles wrong is the one mistake you can't recover from in this loop.

Master's thesis, 2017 — The Effect of Interruptions During a Laparoscopy Skills Training Task

Dissertation, 2020 — The Effects of Interruption Relevance and Complexity on Primary Task Resumption and Mental Demand

Both under Mark Scerbo, at Old Dominion's Simulation Usability Research Facility. Your dissertation committee included Kara Latorella from NASA Langley — she's the aviation-interruption researcher, and that's a clean bridge if your NASA internship comes up.

Why you lead with it

It's your strongest credential for this job, and it is not on the résumé they're reading. If you wait for them to find it, they never will. Get it out inside the first sixty seconds.

The sixty-second core — this is the whole opener

About a hundred and fifteen words. Say it, then stop talking.

"I spent five years on one question: what an interruption actually costs someone doing medical work. My master's thesis put it inside a laparoscopic skills trainer, and that became an HFES paper. My dissertation moved it into a controlled medical data-entry task so I could isolate what drives the cost.

Thirty-six people, twenty-seven interruptions each, every one exactly eighteen seconds. Difficulty dominated: once an interruption demanded working memory, resumption errors roughly tripled. And a reminder only helped when it pointed at the exact place you'd left.

The lesson I still design around: getting the user back to where they were is the system's job, not the user's."

Everything below this point is a follow-up. Use it when they invite it. Volunteering all of it is how a strong opener turns into a lecture.

Follow-up · the three implications

Your sixty-second core already lands the first of these. Bring the other two out when they ask what the work means for design.

"Three things came out of it that I still design around.

First, getting back to where you were is the system's job, not the user's. Thirty of my thirty-six participants were rehearsing — holding their place in their head — and rehearsal failed them exactly when the interruption got hard. If the user's own strategy collapses under load, the system owes them the state back.

Second, interruptions belong in the use specification, not in the error term. The same task carries different risk when it's interrupted. That makes it a characteristic of the use environment, and it belongs in your hazard-related use scenarios like any other condition.

And third, a reminder only helps if it's the exact thing they were doing. Related-but-wrong bought nothing — people told me afterward they ignored the cue unless it matched the precise field they'd left. The specificity is the whole mechanism."

Follow-up · if a peer pushes on method

Three answers carry the weight. About thirty seconds each.

The competing theories. They may ask what the study was actually testing between.

"The three theories were [name 1], [name 2], and [name 3]. The data separated them because [one sentence — which prediction held and which one failed]."

⚠️ Fill this in before you use the word "theories" anywhere. If you can't name all three and say in one sentence how the data told them apart, cut the reference entirely and describe the findings on their own. A half-remembered theory name is worse than no theory name.

How you removed the shortcuts. Say this one slowly — it's the strongest thing in the document.

"When the interruption ended, I wiped everything they'd already entered and reset the cursor to the top-left corner. That mattered because the two studies closest to mine left the cursor sitting where the person had been, or left the instructions visible on screen — so their participants could have been reading the answer off the display rather than remembering it. I took away every visual and spatial cue, so the only thing left was memory. Which is what the measure was supposed to be about in the first place."

Why it transfers.

"It transfers as a mechanism, not as a number. Resumption cost is a working-memory property. What changes in an operating room is how often it happens, what it costs when it does, and whether a team absorbs it. So I'd carry it forward as a hypothesis with a formative study attached — never as a threshold."

If they want the numbers. Difficulty on resumption time: F(2,70) = 29.12, p < .001, partial η² = .46. Reminder condition: F(2,70) = 5.60, p = .006. The interaction came out null and underpowered, around .6 — say that plainly if asked. The debriefs suggest people ignored both of the unhelpful reminders, so the mechanism it depended on never engaged.

⚠️ Verify every figure against the dissertation PDF before you say any of them out loud. These came out of a summary, not the document. A number you can't defend under a follow-up is worse than no number.

The thesis is your "a study went wrong" story ★ don't waste it

When someone asks about a failure, this is the answer.

⚠️ Reread the HFES 2018 paper first. It's published, it's findable, and it's co-authored. Make sure this telling matches how the paper actually framed the results — a published version of the study you're narrating is the one document an interviewer can check against you in real time.

"My master's thesis was supposed to measure how long it takes to get back to a laparoscopic task after an interruption. The setup was the standard laparoscopic skills exercise — moving six colored rings in a memorized order through a trainer box. And I interrupted people the way the literature says surgeons actually get interrupted: somebody talks to you about something unrelated. [author, year] found that accounts for about a quarter of all interruptions in an operating room.

Forty-six of my fifty-one participants never stopped. They just kept moving rings while they answered me. So they were distracted, not interrupted — and the thing I was measuring stopped existing. A six-person pilot had shown me the opposite.

What I actually learned is that interruption and distraction are different things, and which one you get isn't a property of the stimulus. It's a property of the person and the moment. I'd designed it as though it were a property of the stimulus. That was the error.

I wrote the fix into the document — use a visual blackout instead of a conversation, which forces a real suspension and gives you the exact interval. That's the design the dissertation ended up using.

And it's why I now pilot the manipulation check separately from the effect, and why I get people to agree up front on what each result would change. Six people told me my manipulation worked. Fifty-one told me it didn't."

Follow-up · why this matters at Intuitive specifically

Use this when they ask why surgical robotics, or what drew you to the domain. Don't front-load it.

"The literature I was working in is the operating room. [author, year] found an interruption roughly every six minutes in general and orthopedic surgery. [author, year] counted twenty-six in a single gynecological procedure, with about eighty percent of them hitting the whole team. And in a simulated gallbladder removal, [author, year] found that realistic interruptions took the major error rate from six percent up to forty-four.

Now put that next to a console surgeon, who is visually isolated from the room. Essentially every piece of communication from the bedside reaches that person as an interruption. That's a design problem. And it happens to be the one I've spent the most time on."

⚠️ Fill in the citations, or soften every sentence to "one study found." Both are fine. What isn't fine is quoting a precise statistic you can't attribute — in this room, several people will know this literature, and being caught on a number you borrowed costs you more than the number ever bought.

The trap: this is the most interesting thing you'll say all day, which is exactly why it will run long. Ninety seconds. Then stop.

Story 2 · The latency threshold (Amazon)

The reframe. You've told this before as a "requirements" story. Here it's a design input story — a claim about human perception, measured properly, and written down in a form an engineer can test against.

"Alexa had latency targets that were basically round numbers somebody liked. My argument was that a latency target is a claim about human perception — so it should be measured like one, and written down like one.

So we [defined the measurement moment precisely / measured detection thresholds adaptively / carried the uncertainty into the spec / chose a population percentile deliberately] — not 'system latency,' but the gap between the person's input and the first feedback they get, in a named channel.

What came out wasn't a report. It was an acceptance criterion an engineer could verify: feedback within this interval, for this share of users, under worst-case system load, with a defined fallback if the hardware can't get there. The economics team applied those thresholds and projected about fifty million dollars in operational value."

⚠️ Keep only the ones you actually did. Each of those four is a specific methodological claim, and each invites a different follow-up. Saying all four when you did two is how a good story turns into an audit.

One clarification to have ready: "worst-case system load" means system load, not operator workload. The study held operator workload constant. Say so if asked — that distinction is the kind of precision that reads as real.

Then the limit — before they ask for it:

"The boundary is real. Thresholds are specific to the channel and the workload. A visual feedback threshold from a consumer device does not port to a gloved, sterile, high-tempo console. The method ports. The number doesn't."

Then build the bridge yourself:

"That's the same shape as force feedback. How much force difference can a surgeon actually tell apart? At what delay does force feedback stop feeling like it's coming from the instrument? And what do you do when the hardware can't deliver it? Those are all psychophysics questions, and they all turn into design inputs and entries in the risk analysis."

If they ask: "and when the hardware can't hit your number?"

"Then you substitute a channel — you don't relax the number. If the visual path can't confirm in time, you acknowledge in a faster channel, so the person still feels the system responded while the real answer resolves. What degrades is completeness in that first window. And that's a documented residual risk with somebody's name against it, not an omission."

Story 5 · The time you pushed back

The job description literally says negotiate design recommendations. This is the senior-level question, and they will ask it.

Four beats:

Set it up honestly. The recommendation, what it cost — schedule, money, performance — and who pushed back. Then grant their objection out loud. A researcher who thinks engineering was simply wrong reads as junior.

Say what you argued with. Best to worst: the risk analysis and the severity of the harm · converging evidence from more than one source · a cheaper alternative you built for them · escalation. Name which you used, and in what order.

Name what you conceded. There's always something. If there isn't, you weren't negotiating — you were waiting.

End on what changed structurally, so the argument didn't have to happen again.

The line that sets your register:

"I don't win these by being right. I win them by making the residual risk visible enough that accepting it requires a signature."

Story 6 · The deal you make before the study ★ your signature habit

"Before a study runs, I get the stakeholders to agree in writing what each possible result will change. If no plausible result changes a decision, I don't run the study."

Why it works, if they ask: it turns a findings meeting into a decision that was already made. Findings get ignored when nobody ever owned the decision. They don't get ignored when the owner pre-committed in writing.

Where it lands here: on a development program, this is how human factors holds a gate. The formative plan says if people fail this task at this rate, the design changes — and here's the alternative we've already scoped.

The beat that makes it read as judgment rather than process: it kills your own studies too. Say that part.

The example. The latency program is the natural one. This story dies if it stays abstract, so fill the slots with what actually happened.

"Before the latency study ran, I got engineering and product to agree in writing on what each result would do. If the measured threshold came in [tighter] than the current target, the target changes. If it came in [looser], we stop spending cycles on latency and move that effort to [X].

It came in [result], and [what changed] — without anyone relitigating whether the research counted, because that argument had happened [months] earlier, before anyone had a result to defend.

And it cuts both ways. I've cancelled my own studies when no plausible result would have changed a decision: [one example]."

Name both branches. That's what proves it was a real pre-commitment and not a prediction you happened to get right. And the "cuts both ways" example has to be a study you actually killed — it's the line that makes this read as judgment rather than process, and it only works if it's concrete.

Part 4 · The haptics question — and anything like it

They handed you a force-feedback hypothetical in the first conversation. Expect another one on the loop.

Don't answer with a study plan. Answer with a sequence — and say the sequence out loud before you use it, so they can follow you:

"Let me take that in order. What already exists, what's already known, what I'd ask experts, what I'd test once there's something to test, and what it all has to turn into."

The three-minute version

First, what already exists. (about 30 seconds)

"There are three reference points already, and between them they bound the problem.

Open surgery, where the surgeon feels tissue directly and their whole model is built on that. Laparoscopic, where force arrives through a long instrument and a pivot — attenuated and distorted. Surgeons have been adapting to a degraded version of that signal for decades. And current robotic practice, where surgeons compensate visually: they read how the tissue deforms instead of feeling resistance.

So the first question isn't 'do surgeons want force feedback.' It's 'what is that visual compensation strategy actually doing for them, and what does restoring force change about it?'"

Second, mine what's already known. (about 25 seconds)

"Before I generate anything, I'd go through the complaint data and the known use problems on the fielded system, plus the literature. Known use problems are free evidence, and they're the cheapest source of hypotheses I have. They're also a required input to the process, so that work has to happen anyway."

Third, go to the experts — before there's anything to test. (about 45 seconds)

"Cognitive task analysis with expert surgeons, about how they work now. Not 'would you like force feedback,' but: what cues are you actually using, when would force information have changed a decision, and where do you currently feel blind.

I'd anchor those in specific remembered cases rather than generalities, because that's where the real reasoning comes out. Contextual inquiry in the OR. And I'd map the workflow across the whole team, not just the console — the bedside assistant and the scrub are users too."

Fourth, once there's something to react to, evaluate it. (about 30 seconds)

"Now it's cognitive walkthroughs, then formative testing at increasing fidelity — each round scoped to a decision we agreed on in advance."

Fifth, close on what it produces. (about 30 seconds)

"The output isn't a report. It's a set of verifiable design inputs — a detectable force difference, a latency bound, a defined behavior when feedback drops out — plus the first entries in the use-related risk analysis. And those early entries are what the submission gets argued from, years later."

⚠️ One precision point. You blurred this once — don't do it again.

Cognitive task analysis studies how experts think in current practice. No prototype needed. It generates.

Cognitive walkthroughs and formative testing evaluate a design. They need something to look at. They evaluate.

Using them interchangeably tells a specialist you learned the vocabulary rather than the practice.

How a need becomes a design input — say this chain out loud

This is the single most valuable thing you can say in the loop, because it's exactly what the job description means by authoring usability design inputs.

"Here's how that chain actually runs — and I'll make the numbers up, because the point is the shape.

Observation: surgeons tell me they're uncertain about grip on friable tissue — vessel wall, bowel — and they compensate by watching how it deforms.

Need: the user has to be able to tell a safe grip from an unsafe one without taking their eyes off the operative field.

Design input: the system shall present force information such that a one-newton difference is discriminable within a hundred and fifty milliseconds of contact, without requiring a visual fixation shift.

And the risk analysis entry. The hazard is mechanical force. The sequence of events: the surgeon can't perceive grip force, so grip escalates. The hazardous situation: a vessel or bowel wall exposed to force beyond what it tolerates. The harm: laceration or bleeding. The critical task is grip application during grasping and retraction. And the candidate risk control is the force display, with its effectiveness verified in formative evaluation before the first integrated prototype review.

That's the whole path, from a thing somebody said to a thing an engineer can verify."

Say "I'll make the numbers up" out loud. It lets you be concrete without claiming to know their thresholds — and being concrete is the entire value of this answer.

Negative transfer Experienced robotic surgeons have trained the visual compensation strategy. New force cues may interfere with it Expert versus novice performance gap. Say this one — it's the counterintuitive one and it shows you've thought past the obvious
Habituation Sensitivity drops over a long case Detection performance early versus late in the procedure
Losing it mid-case Feedback dropping out is a mode change the user may not notice Whether they detect the loss, and performance in the unannounced-loss condition

The trade-off to argue both ways

They may ask whether you'd just put a force number on the screen instead.

"They have different workload profiles, not different quality levels.

A visual force indicator is cheaper, easier to verify, and degrades gracefully — but it spends the visual channel, which is already the bottleneck in the operative field, and it costs an attention shift.

True haptics uses a channel that isn't loaded and needs no attention shift — but it brings latency, calibration, and the risk of disagreeing with what the eyes are saying.

Which one is right depends on where the surgeon's spare capacity actually is during that specific critical task. And that's an empirical question I'd want answered before the architecture gets chosen, not after."

Part 5 · The regulatory picture

⚠️ Read this first. You are not the regulatory person, and pretending to be is the fastest way to lose the room. Frame everything here as current reading, not expertise: "I've been working through the two 2026 guidances." And when you hit something you don't know: "I'd want to read the clause before I answered that." That sentence costs you nothing and buys more than a guess.

Also: say guidance, never "regulation." They're different things and the difference matters to these people.

The big picture — two documents, one pair

FDA refreshed its entire human factors framework inside a single year. There are two documents and they now work as a matched set.

The May 2026 one — Content of Human Factors Information in Medical Device Marketing Submissions. Announced May 29, 2026. It tells you what to put in a submission, and it covers 510(k)s, De Novo requests, PMAs, and HDE applications.

The August 2026 one — Applying Human Factors and Usability Engineering to Medical Devices. Revised August 3, 2026 — the first revision since 2016. It tells you how to do the work.

The date to know: August 1, 2026. Submissions received before then aren't expected to include the newly recommended content, and the revised eSTAR templates began prompting for the HF Submission Category on that date.

The observation to open with — it makes you sound current instead of rehearsed:

"What I find interesting is that they're a matched pair now. Section 9 of the August revision stopped describing what to submit and just points at the May guidance. One document is how you do the work, the other is what you hand over. Teams that only read one of them either do good work and present it badly, or present a tidy package built on a weak process."

The three categories, and the one decision that matters

Every submission now lands in one of three categories, decided by a four-step flowchart.

Category 1 — a modification with no change to the user interface, the users, the uses, the use environments, the training, or the labeling. You submit a written justification that human factors is unaffected, plus a conclusion and a high-level summary.

Category 2 — either there are no critical tasks, or the modification doesn't touch any — or critical tasks are affected but validation testing isn't warranted. You submit a written rationale plus a defined subset of the report: your conclusion, a description of users and uses and environments and training, a description of the user interface, and a summary of known use problems. For a modified device, you also owe a comparative risk analysis.

Category 3 — new or affected critical tasks, and validation testing is warranted. All eight sections, including the full risk analysis, the critical task list, and the complete validation study with its protocol attached.

Decision Point D is the one worth knowing cold. It's where you decide whether affected critical tasks still need validation testing — and the rationale rests on three things:

  1. An established history of safe use for that user interface
  2. Low user interface complexity
  3. The continued adequacy of existing risk controls

That accommodation is new in the final version — the 2022 draft didn't offer it. And it's exactly the judgment call a senior human factors analyst gets handed.

Two traps worth naming out loud, because they catch people:

  • Labeling and training are inside the user interface. So a labeling-only change does not get you Category 1. People get this wrong constantly.
  • Expanding an indication sends you back through the flowchart even with no device change — and with a new user population, it will often land in Category 3.

What actually changed in August

FDA's own change table lists three things.

One — the definitions got updated and expanded. New entries for harm, normal use, residual risk, serious harm, serious injury, use environment, and use-related risk analysis. Two definitions got notably broader: a task is now "one or more user interactions with a medical device to achieve a desired result," and the user interface is now explicitly everything the user interacts through — packaging, labeling, training materials, controls, displays, alarms, and the logic of the device itself. And throughout, "risk management measure" became "risk control measure."

Two — Section 9, the documentation section, got much shorter and now points at the May guidance. It says your risk management records, your testing, and your design optimization records — the design and development file — are the evidence.

Three — Appendix A was deleted. That's the familiar eight-section report outline everyone used. It now lives in the May guidance instead.

What did not change — this is where your credibility actually lives

Anyone can recite what's new. Knowing what held is the tell that you understand the framework.

Critical tasks are prioritized by the severity of the potential harm, not by probability — because the probability of a use error can't be estimated reliably. That one sentence is the load-bearing logic of the entire framework. Say it.

FDA's validation expectations held: generally at least fifteen participants per distinct user group, representative US users, a production-equivalent interface with final labeling, and no fixing an observed use error with labeling or training alone without evidence that the fix works. Deviations need justification. And a pre-submission is the route for borderline Category 2-versus-3 calls.

The three standards, in one paragraph each

QMSR. The amended 21 CFR Part 820, which now incorporates ISO 13485 by reference. It took effect February 2, 2026. The practical consequence for you is vocabulary: say "design and development file," never DHF.

IEC 62366-1. The process chain, in order: use specification → user interface characteristics related to safety → known use problems → hazard-related use scenarios → critical tasks → evaluation plan → formative evaluation → validation. Two lines worth having:

"The chain is the traceability argument. Every critical task has to have come from somewhere, and every risk control has to get evaluated."

"Formative isn't a small summative. It's the only place the design actually changes."

ISO 14971. Two things, held exactly. The causal chain is hazard → sequence of events → hazardous situation → harm, in that order. Most people collapse hazard and hazardous situation; not collapsing them is an instant credibility marker. And the control hierarchy is design it out → protect against it → tell people about it.

The point that ties it back to your actual job

This is the one to land, because it connects the regulatory frame to the 0→1 work they're hiring for.

"What interests me about this is the timing. The submission category, and the argument for whether you needed validation testing, get settled years downstream. But they're settled by the risk analysis and the formative record somebody created at the very beginning.

If that early use-related risk analysis is thin, you end up arguing from a weak record at the worst possible moment. So I treat early generative work as the first draft of the submission argument — not as pre-work."
