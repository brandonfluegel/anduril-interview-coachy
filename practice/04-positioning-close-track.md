# 04 — Positioning, Scope & Close Track (PQ01–PQ10)

The frame around everything else: the pitch, the level argument, the objection, and the close. These decide how the panel *reads* your technical and behavioral answers.

**Targets:** opening pitch 210–230 words ≈ 90–100s · positioning answers 150–200 words ≈ 65–85s · objection handling 120–160 words ≈ 60s. Follow-ups 45–90 words.

**The only open item is level.** Location, compensation, travel, and clearance were settled in the recruiter screen. Never raise them. Level is argued from **scope owned**, never from title held.

---

## PQ01 — Opening Positioning Pitch

**Arc:** opening, close · **Target:** 90–100s · **This is the single highest-leverage answer in the loop.** You will give it four times.

> **Base:** Walk me through your background. Ninety seconds.

### Model answer — memorize this one verbatim (~215 words / 92s)

> I'm a human factors engineer and researcher, and the work I've cared about is consistently the work where a use error is irreversible.
>
> Ph.D. in Human Factors Psychology — my dissertation was on working memory and reaction time under high-stress task interruption. Six years applied since then.
>
> Three things I'd point at.
>
> At Amazon I ran a multi-year psychophysics program that replaced arbitrary engineering latency targets with human perceptual thresholds. It informed about $50 million in operational value, and the multimodal interaction architecture became US Patent US-12532040-B1.
>
> At NASA Langley I did human-systems integration for Lunar Gateway clinical workstations — a use-error analysis against NASA-STD-3001 and MIL-STD-1472. Task time down 30%, critical input errors eliminated. And the errors were eliminated by physical layout, not by speed.
>
> At Sling I'm Staff, and I own human factors across software, hardware, and AI — reach envelopes and mechanical fit specs on one side, and on the other I wrote *Principles for Agentic Trust*, accepted to CSCW 2026, for auditing systems whose behavior isn't deterministic.
>
> What I want to be known for is one claim: in autonomous systems the goal isn't the fastest interaction, it's the best-authorized one. I call that Calibrated Cognitive Friction. It's a thesis — I haven't deployed it.
>
> Air Defense is where that question is a live engineering problem instead of a paper. That's why I'm here.

### Follow-ups

**F1 — "You just gave me a career history. Give it to me again as an argument for why you are the right person for this specific role."** *(~80 words)*

> Three claims. One: I've done human-systems integration on safety-critical systems against military and NASA standards, and I know where those standards stop being enough. Two: I turn human evidence into specifications with acceptance tests, not recommendations — that's what the Amazon latency work actually was. Three: I've built the audit framework for non-deterministic systems, which is the specific gap between conventional usability method and autonomous command and control. The thing I don't have is defense domain experience, and I'd rather name that than talk around it.

**F2 — "What is the one sentence you want me to repeat about you when I debrief with the panel?"** *(~40 words)*

> "He turns human limits into specifications engineers can verify — and he's the one person who'll tell you when the friction he's advocating for isn't worth it." That second half is the part I'd want said, because it's what makes the first half usable.

**F3 — "You have a Ph.D. and a patent. Which of those actually matters for Air Defense, and why?"** *(~65 words)*

> The Ph.D., and not for the credential. What it bought me was psychophysical method — how to establish a human threshold defensibly with a small sample, which is exactly the constraint in this domain. The patent matters as evidence that the work reached a system architecture rather than a report. But if you took one away, the method is what I'd keep.

### Senior → Lead/Staff

- **Senior:** recites a competent chronological history.
- **Lead/Staff:** opens with a thesis, supports it with three proof points, and lands on why this role is the next move — in under ninety seconds.
- **Structural test:** if your pitch could be reordered without loss, it's a history. If reordering breaks it, it's an argument.

## PQ02 — Why Leave, Why Here, Why Now

**Arc:** opening, midpoint · **Target:** ~70s

> **Base:** You started at Sling in 2025 as a Staff Human Factors Engineer. Why leave, and why this role?

### Model answer (~175 words / 70s)

**Direct on the awkward part first.** Short tenure and a posted title one step below mine are both real, and I'd rather address them than have you infer.

**On the title:** I'm not optimizing for it. What I'm optimizing for is the consequence class of the problem. I'd take a differently-titled role on this problem over a Staff title on a lower-stakes one, and I think that's the honest ordering of my preferences.

**On leaving:** nothing is wrong at Sling. I built the human factors function there from a standing start and it's real — the criteria, the specs, the Agentic Trust framework. What I don't have is a domain where the authorization question is load-bearing. At Sling, Calibrated Cognitive Friction stays a thesis, because nothing there makes it urgent enough to test properly.

**Why now:** the human-control patterns for autonomous systems are being set right now, and defaults calcify once operators have trained on them. Being three years late to that is being late in a way you can't recover.

### Follow-ups

**F1 — "What would have to be true here for you to still be in this role in four years?"** *(~65 words)*

> That the thesis got tested rather than just admired — that at some point we ran the study that could have killed it. And that the scope compounded: standards and mechanisms I own, not a longer list of studies I ran. If in four years I'm still running individual studies well, I'll have stayed too long, and I'd rather say that now than discover it then.

**F2 — "What is the honest answer about what is not working where you are now?"** *(~65 words)*

> Nothing is broken, which is the honest and slightly unsatisfying answer. The real gap is that the hardest question I work on isn't urgent there. A framework for agentic trust in a domain where the consequences are recoverable will always be a good idea rather than a requirement, and good ideas don't get tested. I want to be somewhere it's a requirement.

**F3 — "What specifically about counter-drone air defense pulled you, as opposed to any other defense program?"** *(~65 words)*

> The time constant. Most systems let you design a genuinely deliberative human step because the timeline permits it. Counter-drone compresses that until you have to decide in advance what the human is actually *for* — it becomes structural rather than procedural. That's precisely the question my work is about, and there are very few places where it's an engineering problem right now instead of a paper.

### Senior → Lead/Staff

- **Senior:** gives a credible, non-negative reason for moving.
- **Lead/Staff:** frames the move around a mission and a scope of influence they intend to own, and handles the tenure and title questions without a flicker of defensiveness.
- **Say this:** *"I'm not moving for a title. I'm moving because the question I work on is load-bearing here and it isn't where I am."*

## PQ03 — First 90 Days & Entry Plan

**Arc:** midpoint, close · **Target:** ~80s

> **Base:** Say you start next month. What are you doing in your first ninety days, and what have you shipped by day ninety?

### Model answer (~195 words / 78s)

**Days 1–30 — the constraint layer.** I'd map the operator workflow and the authority boundaries: who decides what, where control transfers, what's reversible and what isn't. I'd get to a field or training environment as early as anyone will let me, because that's the thing I can't compress later. And I'd ask each engineering and design partner one question — which document actually gets read — so I know where to write.

**Days 30–60 — one decision, one study.** I'd pick a single live decision, ideally one that's already contested, and run something small enough to land inside the cycle it belongs to. Being useful on a real decision early matters more than being comprehensive.

**Days 60–90 — the artifact.** An operator authority-state map and blueprint for one core workflow: frontstage operator actions, backstage system and autonomy behavior, and the failure and recovery paths. Plus the beginning of an intake — decision, owner, date, consequence class.

**What I'd deliberately not do:** produce an opinion about the current interface in month one just to look useful. And I'd say that out loud, because silence gets read as endorsement.

**Caveat.** I don't know this organization's constraints, so I'd expect this plan to be corrected in week two, and I'd want it to be.

### Follow-ups

**F1 — "What is the single artifact you would ship in that window, and who is forced to use it?"** *(~70 words)*

> The authority-state map and blueprint for one core workflow. Nobody's forced by mandate — it gets used because design needs it to stay consistent, engineering needs it to enumerate modes, and product needs it to sequence. That's the test I'd hold myself to: if it isn't in use by day 120, it was the wrong artifact and I'd say so rather than campaign for it.

**F2 — "What would you deliberately not do in the first ninety days, even though someone will ask for it?"** *(~65 words)*

> Usability testing on the current interface. Someone will ask in week two, it's easy to say yes to, and it produces a list of fixes that makes me look immediately useful and costs me the quarter. I'd rather trade that visible early win for the constraint layer, and tell the person asking exactly why — including that I'll do it in Q2.

**F3 — "How would you know at day ninety that you had picked the wrong first problem?"** *(~65 words)*

> Silence. Not disagreement — disagreement means it's live and connected to something. If nobody's arguing with the authority map by day sixty, it isn't touching a decision anyone is making, and that's reliable feedback. My checkpoint is four weeks in: name the decision this now touches. If I can't, I stop rather than finish it out of momentum.

### Senior → Lead/Staff

- **Senior:** plans a thorough onboarding and a first study.
- **Lead/Staff:** commits to a decision, an artifact, and an operating mechanism by day ninety, and names what they're consciously deferring.
- **Say this:** *"By day ninety I want one artifact in use and one intake running. Not a research plan — a thing people are working from."*

## PQ04 — Research Vision for Air Defense

**Arc:** opening, midpoint · **Target:** ~80s

> **Base:** If you owned Human Factors research for Air Defense, what would you build that does not exist yet?

### Model answer (~195 words / 78s)

**What I'd build.** A trust-calibration measurement capability that runs continuously in production, not in studies. Concretely: log the recommendation, the displayed confidence, the operator's decision, the decision latency, and the outcome — so operator calibration becomes a computable quantity rather than something you infer after an incident.

**Why that one.** Everything else in this domain depends on it. You can't tell whether a confirmation step is earning its cost, you can't tell whether a model update degraded the human-system pair, and you can't tell over-trust from appropriate trust — all of which are currently answered by opinion. This turns them into measurements.

**What it makes possible.** Removing friction with evidence rather than nerve. That's the part I'd sell hardest: this capability is at least as useful for *deleting* controls as for adding them.

**Cost and first version.** It's a small amount of instrumentation and an analysis pipeline — the expensive part is agreeing what gets logged, which is an engineering and policy conversation, not a research one. The one-quarter version is a single workflow, one logged decision point, and a calibration report that tells us whether acceptance varies with confidence at all.

**What I'd stop doing to fund it.** Validation studies on decisions that are already effectively made.

### Follow-ups

**F1 — "What would you have to stop doing to fund that?"** *(~60 words)*

> Validation studies on decisions that are already made. They're comfortable, they make teams feel supported, and they're the least valuable thing a research function does. Killing them is unpopular for about two weeks and then nobody mentions it again. That's the trade, and I'd rather name it than pretend the capability is free.

**F2 — "Who has to agree for that to exist, and how do you get them there?"** *(~65 words)*

> Engineering has to agree to the logging, and whoever owns data policy has to agree to the retention and handling. I get there by making the first version nearly free — one decision point, existing telemetry where possible — and by leading with the deletion case rather than the safety case. "This tells us which confirmations to remove" is a much easier first conversation.

**F3 — "What is the smallest first version of it that proves the idea in one quarter?"** *(~65 words)*

> One workflow, one logged authorization point, and one question: does operator acceptance vary with displayed confidence? If it does, calibration is healthy there and we've built the baseline. If it's flat, we've found over-trust in production with existing instrumentation and no study. Either answer is worth the quarter, which is the property I look for in a first version.

### Senior → Lead/Staff

- **Senior:** proposes better studies.
- **Lead/Staff:** proposes a durable capability, names its owner and cost, and sizes a first version that ships fast.
- **Say this:** *"I'd rather ship one logged field than run three studies. The field keeps answering questions after I've moved on."*

## PQ05 — Level & Scope Argument

**Arc:** midpoint, close · **Target:** ~75s · **This is the one open item in the whole loop. Do not improvise it.**

> **Base:** This requisition is posted at Senior. Make the case for the scope you actually intend to own.

### Model answer (~185 words / 75s)

**Frame it as scope, never as title.** I'm not going to argue about the label. I'll tell you what I intend to own, and if that's Senior here, that's a useful thing for me to learn.

**Three things I'd own that a Senior researcher wouldn't.**

**One — the evidence bar.** Not running rigorous studies; defining what this organization accepts as sufficient evidence for a given class of decision. Directional versus decision-grade versus threshold-grade, written down, so teams buy speed deliberately instead of by accident.

**Two — specifications, not recommendations.** Human limits written as requirements with acceptance tests that hardware and software have to pass. That's what the Amazon latency work was, and it's the difference between influencing a decision and constraining every future one.

**Three — the operating mechanism.** Intake, prioritization, the repository, and a stop-ship threshold agreed before anyone needs it.

**And the honest part.** A Senior researcher answers the questions the program asks. What I'm describing is shaping which questions get asked, and being accountable when the bets are wrong. If the level follows from that, good. I'd rather earn it visibly than negotiate it now.

### Follow-ups

**F1 — "Name the three things you would own that a Senior researcher would not."** *(~60 words)*

> The evidence bar — what counts as sufficient for which class of decision. The specifications — human limits as acceptance criteria engineering has to pass, not recommendations they can weigh. And the operating mechanism — intake, prioritization, and the stop-ship threshold. Studies are the input to all three. None of the three is a study.

**F2 — "What evidence from this conversation supports that scope claim?"** *(~70 words)*

> The latency work is the clearest: the durable output wasn't a threshold, it was a spec format that changed how targets got proposed after I left it. The Agentic Trust framework is the second — I wrote it without a mandate and put it through external review, and it became the shared language. Both are cases of building the thing other people's work has to clear. That's the pattern I'd repeat here.

**F3 — "What would you need to demonstrate in the first six months to make that scope obvious to everyone?"** *(~70 words)*

> One specification that hardware or software actually had to pass, one contested decision changed inside its own cycle, and one mechanism running that I didn't have to enforce personally. If all three exist at six months, the scope argument makes itself and nobody has to have this conversation. If they don't, then the level question was answered correctly at hire and I'd accept that.

### Senior → Lead/Staff

- **Senior:** asks for a title.
- **Lead/Staff:** argues scope — standards owned, decisions changed, mechanisms built — and states what they'd demonstrate to earn it.
- **Never say:** "I'm currently Staff, so..." Title-to-title comparison is the weakest available argument and it invites a comparison of company scale.

## PQ06 — How You Work With Research Leadership

**Arc:** midpoint · **Target:** ~70s

> **Base:** How do you want to be managed, and what do you need from a research leader to do your best work?


### Model answer (~175 words / 70s)

**I'd rather describe a working contract than a management style.**

**What I'll own without being asked:** picking the bets and telling you what I picked before I start, not after. Being accountable when the bet is wrong rather than explaining why it was reasonable.

**What I need from you:** context I can't get myself — where the program is actually going, which decisions are genuinely open, and where I'm about to spend a month on something that's already been decided. That's the single most valuable thing a research leader gives me, and it's the one nobody asks for.

**What I'll escalate rather than absorb:** anything with catastrophic severity and no independent path to catch it. Nothing else. I don't want to be the person who escalates routinely.

**On disagreement:** in the room, directly, once. Then whichever way it lands, I carry the decision and I don't relitigate it in the hallway. If new evidence appears, the evidence reopens it, not me.

**Where I struggle:** I will over-invest in a framework past the point of usefulness. Telling me that early is a favor, not a criticism.

### Follow-ups

**F1 — "Tell me about the best manager relationship you have had and what specifically made it work."** *(~65 words)*

> [Your specific instance.] The property that's mattered most for me is being given problems rather than tasks — a decision that needed to be right, without the method attached. That's also the thing that most clearly signals trust. The second property is being told the political and program context I couldn't see, which prevented more wasted work than any prioritization framework.

**F2 — "How do you raise a problem with your own leadership before it becomes visible to everyone else?"** *(~65 words)*

> Early and small, with a proposed action rather than an alarm. My rule is that my lead should never learn about a problem in my area from someone else — that's a one-strike thing for me. And I try to bring it while it's still boring, because a problem raised while it's boring is a conversation, and the same problem raised late is an incident.

**F3 — "What feedback have you received that you initially rejected and later acted on?"** *(~70 words)*

> [Your specific instance.] The one I'd expect to be true, and which fits the pattern I named: that I over-build frameworks — that a shorter, cruder version would have changed more decisions sooner. I resisted it because rigor felt like the point. It wasn't; the decision was. I now force a usable version out early even when it embarrasses me, and it works better.

### Senior → Lead/Staff

- **Senior:** describes a preferred management style.
- **Lead/Staff:** describes a working contract — what they own unasked, what they escalate, and how disagreement resolves.
- **Say this:** *"You should never hear about a problem in my area from someone else. That's the contract."*

## PQ07 — Cross-Panel Message Discipline

**Arc:** midpoint, close · **Target:** ~70s

> **Base:** You will say this to four different interviewers today. Give me the version of your thesis that an engineer, a PM, and a designer would each accept.

### Model answer (~185 words / 75s)

**The invariant — this sentence does not change in any room:**

> In autonomous systems the goal isn't the fastest interaction, it's the best-authorized one — so friction is a risk-proportional control surface, and it belongs in the spec, not in a deck.

**The engineering version.** Acceptance rate should vary with displayed confidence. If it doesn't, we have over-trust, and that's observable in production telemetry today — no study required. Mandatory confirmation is tiered by reversibility, and a model change that shifts the confidence distribution triggers revalidation.

**The product version.** A confirmation step is an investment with a measurable return. I can tell you which ones are earning their cost and which ones to delete — and I expect to delete more than I add.

**The design version.** Uncertainty has to be a state the operator behaves differently in, not a number in a corner. Variation is by consequence class, and the rule is consistent even when the pattern isn't.

**What stays identical:** the claim about authorization quality, and the commitment that it's falsifiable. What changes is only the evidence and the consequence each partner cares about. If the claim itself changes, that's drift, and you'll catch it in debrief tonight.

### Follow-ups

**F1 — "Which part of that message do you catch yourself changing depending on the audience, and is that translation or drift?"** *(~70 words)*

> The part I have to watch is the certainty. With engineers I get more categorical because they want a threshold; with researchers I get more hedged because hedging is the register. That's drift, not translation, and it's the one I actively correct — the confidence level in a claim should be a property of the evidence, not of the room. Evidence and consequence can change. Certainty shouldn't.

**F2 — "What is the shortest defensible version — one sentence?"** *(~35 words)*

> Friction at irreversible decision boundaries is a control surface, not a defect — and I can tell you when it isn't worth it.

**F3 — "If two of us compare notes tonight, what should we both have heard?"** *(~60 words)*

> That the goal is the best-authorized action, not the fastest one; that I treat it as falsifiable and told you what would kill it; and that I named the boundary of my own evidence — that Calibrated Cognitive Friction is a thesis I haven't deployed. If one of you heard a deployed result and the other heard a thesis, that's on me.

### Senior → Lead/Staff

- **Senior:** tailors tone to the audience.
- **Lead/Staff:** keeps one invariant claim across all four conversations and changes only the evidence and consequence each partner cares about.
- **Drill this one hardest.** Say the invariant sentence out loud four times, once per persona, back to back. If the wording drifts, the panel debrief will surface it as inconsistency rather than as adaptability.

## PQ08 — Portfolio Framing

**Arc:** opening, close · **Target:** ~75s

> **Base:** Which two pieces of your work show that research changed both a tactical and a strategic decision, and why those two?

### Model answer (~190 words / 76s)

**Tactical — NASA Langley.** Lunar Gateway clinical workstations. I ran a use-error analysis against NASA-STD-3001 and MIL-STD-1472 and it changed a specific design: physical layout and control-display placement. Task time down 30%, critical input errors eliminated. That's tactical in the sense that it changed one system — and the reason I lead with it here is the mechanism. The errors were eliminated by geometry, by putting distance and dissimilarity between controls whose consequences differed. Not by a confirmation dialog and not by speed.

**Strategic — Amazon.** The psychophysics latency program. It didn't change one decision; it changed how a class of decisions got made. Arbitrary engineering targets were replaced with human perceptual thresholds — roughly $50M in operational value, and the multimodal interaction architecture became US Patent US-12532040-B1. But the durable part was the spec format: after that, proposing a latency target meant stating a percentile, a modality, and a fallback.

**Why those two.** Together they're the argument. One shows I can change a physical system with a use-error analysis; the other shows I can change the criteria the organization uses, which is the part that keeps operating after I've gone.

### Follow-ups

**F1 — "What in that work was yours specifically, and what was the team's?"** *(~70 words)*

> At NASA the use-error analysis structure and the layout redesign recommendations were mine; the workstation itself and the broader human-systems integration program were a team's, and I was an intern on it — I want that stated rather than implied. At Amazon the psychophysical method, the threshold derivation, and the spec format were mine. The $50M is a program figure my work informed, not a number I produced alone.

**F2 — "What would you cut from that story if you had eight minutes instead of thirty?"** *(~60 words)*

> The method detail. Eight minutes gets the decision that changed and the mechanism that made it change — geometry eliminated the errors; the spec format outlived me. What I would not cut is the boundary on the numbers, because a caveat dropped for time is indistinguishable from a caveat dropped for convenience.

**F3 — "Which piece is your weakest, and why do you still lead with it?"** *(~70 words)*

> Calibrated Cognitive Friction, and it's not close. It's a thesis with no deployment evidence — I've never run the study that would test it. I still lead with it because it's the position I'd be hired to advance, and because a candidate who only presents finished work is telling you what they've done rather than what they'd do. But I say "thesis" every time I say it.

### Senior → Lead/Staff

- **Senior:** presents strong project work.
- **Lead/Staff:** curates the work as an argument for a level, separates tactical from strategic explicitly, and is precise about personal versus team attribution.
- **Say this:** *"One changed a system. The other changed how systems got specified. The second one is the job I'm asking for."*

## PQ09 — Questions For The Interviewer

**Arc:** close · **Target:** 2–3 questions per persona, and *listen* to the answers

> **Base:** We have a few minutes left. What questions do you have for me?

**The bar here:** a Lead/Staff question reveals a point of view and treats the answer as decision-relevant. Anything you could ask any company is a wasted turn. Ask two, not five — and follow up on the answer rather than moving to the next item on your list.

### Ask any of these

> "Where does research authority actually sit when evidence and schedule conflict? Is there a decision a researcher holds outright here, or is the function advisory by design? Either is workable — I'd just want to know which."

> "What research has this team decided *not* to do? That tells you more about a research culture's maturity than what it has done."

> "Where does the team knowingly carry human-factors risk right now — the thing everyone accepts and nobody has time for?"

> "What's the last human-factors constraint that actually changed a design or an architecture here — and what made that one land?"

> "What's the human-factors decision this team has made that you're least sure about?"

**One role-specific opener each, if you want it:** *Research* — "what's your bar between publication-quality and decision-grade evidence?" *Engineering* — "what's observable today about operator decisions in production?" *Product* — "what's the most expensive thing you've shipped and had to unwind?" *Design* — "where do you want research to stop?"

### Follow-ups

**F1 — "That is a question you could ask any company. What do you actually want to know about this one?"** *(~60 words)*

> Fair. Then: what's the human-factors decision this team has made that you're least sure about? Not the biggest challenge — the specific call where you'd want a second opinion. That tells me whether I'd be doing work that's genuinely open, and it's the closest thing to a preview of my first six months.

**F2 — "What answer to that question would make you less interested in the role?"** *(~65 words)*

> If the answer to "what research have you decided not to do" is "we do whatever gets requested." That would tell me the function is a service desk, and I'd be the wrong hire for it — I'd take up capacity arguing for an agenda nobody wanted. I'd rather find that out today than in month four.

**F3 — "What is the question you are hesitant to ask?"** *(~65 words)*

> The level one. The req is posted at Senior and I've been arguing scope rather than title all day, which I think is the right way round — but I'd genuinely like to know whether the scope I described reads as this role or as a different one. I'd rather have that answered plainly than leave and guess at it.

### Senior → Lead/Staff

- **Senior:** asks informed, respectful questions.
- **Lead/Staff:** asks questions that reveal a point of view — where research authority sits, what the team refuses to do, how evidence and velocity trade — and reacts to the answer.
- **Do this:** after their answer, say what it *means for you*. "That's useful — it means I'd spend my first quarter on X rather than Y." That single move converts a Q&A into a working conversation.

## PQ10 — Objection Handling & Close

**Arc:** midpoint, close · **Target:** 60s. **Shortest answer in the loop. Do not over-explain.**

> **Base:** The strongest argument against hiring you is that you have never worked in defense. What is your answer?

### Model answer (~150 words / 60s)

**Name it precisely.** It's true, and the version of it that actually matters isn't "no defense experience" — it's that I've never sat with an operator whose judgment was formed under fire. I can read about that and I won't have it. That's a real gap and it takes field time, not study.

**Then the boundary of what transfers.** What does transfer: human-systems integration against MIL-STD-1472 and NASA-STD-3001, use-error analysis on safety-critical systems, and psychophysical method for setting thresholds defensibly with small samples. What does not transfer: any intuition about tempo, tolerance for risk, or what a trained operator will do under adversarial pressure. I'd hold every prior number as a hypothesis to re-test here.

**Then the plan and the detection mechanism.** Field time in the first sixty days, and I'd want the first person to tell me I'm wrong about operators to be an operator, not a stakeholder. If at ninety days I'm still reasoning from consumer and automotive analogies, that's the signal I haven't closed it, and it'll be visible.

### Follow-ups

**F1 — "What is the first thing you would be wrong about here, and how would you find out fast?"** *(~65 words)*

> Tempo. I'd almost certainly under-estimate how compressed the real timeline is, which would make my friction proposals too expensive — my thesis is most likely to fail on cost, not on principle. I'd find out by watching, early, and by asking operators what they'd have skipped. That's a sixty-day question, and it's the one I'd want answered before I proposed anything.

**F2 — "Which of your transferable claims does not transfer? Be specific."** *(~70 words)*

> The Mercedes handover numbers. Twenty-four percent on safety and trust ratings came from drivers, in a simulator, with no adversary and a failure mode they were personally inside. The *mechanism* transfers — multimodal pre-alerting reduces re-orientation cost, because that's a property of attention. The magnitude doesn't, and the population doesn't. I'd cite that study for the mechanism and never for the number.

**F3 — "If we hire you and it does not work out, what will the reason have been?"** *(~70 words)*

> That I spent too long building the framework and not enough time being useful on the decisions in front of the team — that's my known failure mode and this environment punishes it harder than my last one did. The second most likely reason is that I misjudged tempo and became the person adding cost. Both are detectable by month four if someone is willing to tell me.

### Senior → Lead/Staff

- **Senior:** deflects the objection with transferable-skills language.
- **Lead/Staff:** names the real gap precisely, states the boundary of what transfers, and gives a concrete plan with a detection mechanism.
- **Say this:** *"I'd rather tell you exactly where my evidence stops than have you find it in month three."*
