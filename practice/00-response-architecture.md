## 1. Two answer structures, not one

You are scored on the Situation-Task-Action-Result format only for behavioral and experience answers. Technical, methodological, and research-craft answers are scored on how you reason, not on how you narrate. Using the wrong one is a relevance penalty even when the content is right.

### Structure A — for behavioral, experience, and cross-functional questions

| Beat | Words | What it must do |
|---|---|---|
| Situation | 20–30 | One sentence. The organization, the stakes, why it was hard. |
| Task | 15–25 | Your specific charge, in first person. |
| Action | 90–120 | "I," not "we." The hardest moment is the centerpiece. Name what you decided against. |
| Result | 25–40 | Quantified where the evidence supports it. Attribution honest. |
| Earned secret | 20–30 | The thing you know that most people in your role do not. |
| Mechanism | 20–30 | The standard, review gate, or artifact you left behind so the problem does not recur. |

Total: **200–265 words ≈ 90–110 seconds.**

The mechanism beat is the gate between Senior and Lead/Staff. Without it you have described excellent execution, which is the baseline expectation. Say it as its own sentence and make it concrete: *"What I left behind was X, and Y still has to clear it."*

### Structure B — for technical, methodological, and research-craft questions

| Beat | Words | What it must do |
|---|---|---|
| The claim | 20–30 | Lead with the recommendation or the position. No windup. |
| How you would know | 40–60 | The method, or how the effect actually works. |
| Evidence, and where it stops | 40–60 | Your canonical evidence, then its boundary — before anyone asks. |
| The decision it drives | 30–45 | The threshold, gate, or specification this produces. What changes on Monday. |
| What would change your mind | 20–35 | The result that would overturn the claim, and how you would detect it. |

Total: **160–230 words ≈ 65–90 seconds.**

Naming the boundary of your own evidence, and saying what would change your mind, are what separate you from a confident bluffer. Volunteering the limit buys more credibility than any additional proof point.

## 2. Length discipline

| Answer type | Target | Hard ceiling |
|---|---|---|
| Opening 90-second pitch | 210–230 words | 100 seconds |
| Technical main answer | 160–230 words | 90 seconds |
| Behavioral main answer | 200–265 words | 110 seconds |
| Culture / stakeholder answer | 170–220 words | 90 seconds |
| Follow-up probe | 45–90 words | 35 seconds |
| Objection handling | 120–160 words | 60 seconds |

Follow-ups are the highest-risk moment. A follow-up is a scalpel; answer it with a scalpel. Answer the one thing asked, land it, stop. If you re-narrate the original story on a follow-up, you have signaled narrative hoarding and you lose Structure and Relevance points on both answers.

**Voice rule:** ~150 words per minute spoken. Time yourself. If a 90-second answer runs 2:10, the problem is almost always an unnecessary second example, not the level of detail.

## 3. The five uplevel moves

Every strong answer makes at least one. A Strong Hire answer makes two or three. Never force all five.

1. **Decision first.** State the recommendation in sentence one, then earn it.
2. **Consequence link.** Tie evidence to a system, product, or operational outcome — not to a finding.
3. **Alternatives + conditions.** Name what you rejected and what would flip you.
4. **Reusable mechanism.** A standard, threshold, gate, blueprint, intake, or repository someone else uses without you.
5. **Evidence hygiene.** Separate verified from hypothesis out loud, without becoming indecisive.

**Register check (Tone & Authority):** "We ran a study and reported findings" is a 1–2 regardless of program size. "I set the perceptual-threshold spec that latency budgets now have to clear" is a 4–5. Hedges — *"I sort of drove," "they let me," "I was involved in"* — cap you at 2 no matter how good the work was. Delete them from your vocabulary for the day.

## 5. Who is in the room

**Your answer does not change between these four conversations.** The claim, the evidence, and the boundary stay identical — that is what survives a panel debrief. This table is for reading the room and understanding why a follow-up is coming, not for rewriting an answer on the fly.

| Persona | Wants | Currency | Fatal move |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Dr. Daniella Kim** (Research) | Falsifiability, method defensibility, velocity judgment | Method + limits | Abstract framing with no testable claim |
| **Systems / ML Lead** | Implementable, verifiable requirements | Thresholds + failure modes + observability | A recommendation that cannot be instrumented |
| **Product Manager** | Decisions changed, at speed, with ROI | Consequence + cost of being wrong | Sounding like a gate or a bottleneck |
| **Design Lead** | Interaction consequence, physical-digital coherence, partnership | Workflow states + modality + density | Issuing verdicts instead of co-owning |

**One invariant sentence to carry into all four rooms:**

> "In autonomous systems the goal is not the fastest interaction, it is the best-authorized one — so I treat friction as a risk-proportional control surface, and I write it into the spec rather than recommending it in a deck."

## 6. Drill protocol

1. **Cold pass.** Read the question, answer aloud, record. No notes.
2. **Timer pass.** Same question at 60s and at 90s. If the 60s version loses nothing, your 90s version is padded.
3. **Follow-up chain.** Have the app or a partner fire all three follow-ups back to back with no recovery time. This is where sessions actually break.
4. **Phrasing rotation.** Same pillar, all four persona phrasings of the question, in panel order: Kim → Systems/ML → Product → Design. Give the *same answer* each time. You are training recognition, not variation.
5. **Boundary check.** Replay the recording and mark every claim. Canonical, thesis, or invented? Any "invented" is a session failure regardless of score.
