# 05 — NASA Case Study Track (NC01–NC08)

The hiring manager conversation is built around **one** case study. This file is that case study: NASA Langley, Lunar Gateway clinical workstations, human-systems integration under simulated microgravity.

**Why this one and not Amazon.** The Amazon psychophysics program is the bigger number. It is the wrong case for this room. Air Defense hires against *irreversible use error in a constrained environment governed by standards* — and NASA is the only story on your resume where all four of those are true at once. The $50M belongs in the pitch and in follow-ups, not as the spine.

**Targets:** full presentation **380–420 words ≈ 2:35–2:50**. Hard ceiling 3:00. Compression version 150–160 words ≈ 60s. Follow-ups 45–90 words.

**The honest constraint you must own before you open your mouth:** you were a Ph.D. intern there, in 2018, for seven months. Lead/Staff is not argued by inflating that. It is argued by (a) what you *decided*, not what you were assigned, and (b) what you would install as a mechanism today, said explicitly as today's judgment. NC06 is that move. Do not skip it.

## NC01 — The 3-Minute Presentation

**Arc:** opening of the hiring manager loop · **Target:** 2:35–2:50 · **Memorize the shape, not the syllables.**

> **Base:** Walk me through one piece of work end to end. Take about three minutes.

### The beat spine — this is what you actually memorize

| # | Beat | Words | Time | The one job it does |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Claim first** | 35–45 | ~17s | State the finding as a position, before any setup. |
| 2 | **Stakes, not setup** | 40–50 | ~19s | Why a use error here is unrecoverable. Two sentences, no history. |
| 3 | **Your charge** | 25–35 | ~13s | First person. What *you* were on the hook for. |
| 4 | **Method + the hardest moment** | 110–130 | ~50s | uFMEA as a method choice you defend. Name what you rejected. |
| 5 | **Result, attributed honestly** | 40–50 | ~19s | 30% / errors eliminated — and *which* one is the real result. |
| 6 | **Earned secret** | 30–40 | ~15s | The thing most human factors people get backwards here. |
| 7 | **Mechanism — today's version** | 45–55 | ~22s | What you'd install now. This is the level gate. |
| 8 | **Transfer + boundary** | 40–50 | ~19s | Counter-drone, and where the analogy stops. |

Total **~395 words ≈ 2:40**. That leaves twenty seconds of margin for the room. Use it; do not spend it.

### Model script (~400 words / 2:40)

> **[1]** The headline from my NASA work is a finding I didn't expect going in: the task got 30% faster, but the safety result had nothing to do with speed. The critical input errors were eliminated by physical layout — by making the wrong action harder to reach — and the time savings were a side effect. I've designed against that ordering ever since.
>
> **[2]** This was Lunar Gateway clinical workstations at Langley, evaluated in simulated microgravity. The operating constraint is that there's no recovery path. No second operator to catch it, no abort to a benign state, and a body that isn't braced the way every terrestrial workstation assumes. A slip isn't a usability annoyance; it's the outcome.
>
> **[3]** My charge was the human-systems integration validation: determine whether the design would fail against foreseeable *use* error, not component failure, and produce evidence an engineering team could act on.
>
> **[4]** I ran it as a usability Failure Modes and Effects Analysis against NASA-STD-3001 and MIL-STD-1472, rather than as a task-completion usability study. That was the decision I'd defend hardest. A conventional usability study would have given me satisfaction scores and time-on-task, and it would have been genuinely well-run — and it would have measured the wrong thing, because the failures that matter here are rare, high-consequence, and won't show up in any sample I could realistically get in a microgravity rig. So I inverted it: enumerate the use-error modes first, rank them by consequence rather than frequency, and treat the standards as a floor to clear rather than a checklist to pass. [The mode that drove the redesign was `[specific error mode]` — say the concrete one here.] The redesign that came out of it was physical: layout and control-display placement, so the high-consequence action was no longer adjacent to the routine one.
>
> **[5]** Result: 30% reduction in operator task time, and the critical input errors eliminated. I want to be precise about attribution — the layout change is what removed the errors. I was one contributor inside a Langley team, and the analysis and the redesign recommendation are mine.
>
> **[6]** The earned secret: in safety-critical work, speed is not a safety metric. Fluency and error-proneness move together more often than anyone wants to admit, and if you optimize the interaction you frequently make the irreversible action easier to take by accident.
>
> **[7]** What I'd install today, with the scope I have now, isn't a study — it's a gate. Every control surface where an action is irreversible gets a consequence-ranked use-error analysis before layout freezes, and "no high-consequence action adjacent to a high-frequency one" becomes an acceptance criterion the hardware has to clear, not a recommendation I make in a review.
>
> **[8]** That transfers to counter-drone almost directly — irreversible action, compressed timeline, an operator who is physically and cognitively loaded. Where it stops: NASA gave me a bounded task set and minutes to work with. An air defense engagement gives you neither, and the enumeration approach has to become something that runs continuously rather than once before freeze. That's the part I'd want to find out is already solved.

### What each numbered beat is buying you

- **[1]** Uplevel move: *decision first* + it makes the panel curious instead of patient.
- **[4]** Uplevel move: *alternatives + conditions.* Naming the well-run study you rejected is worth more than the study you ran.
- **[5]** Uplevel move: *evidence hygiene.* Volunteering that you were a contributor, before anyone asks, is what makes the rest of your attribution trustworthy.
- **[7]** Uplevel move: *reusable mechanism.* Without [7] this is an excellent Senior answer. With [7] it is a Lead/Staff answer.
- **[8]** Uplevel move: *evidence hygiene* again — naming the boundary of your own analogy before the interviewer does.

### Senior → Lead/Staff

- **Senior:** narrates a well-executed study chronologically, lands the 30%, stops at the result.
- **Lead/Staff:** leads with the counterintuitive finding, defends a *method choice* against a credible alternative, separates the real result from the flattering one, and converts the whole thing into an acceptance criterion someone else can apply without them.
- **Structural test:** delete beat [7]. If the answer is still complete, you told it as a Senior.

## NC03 — Follow-Ups: Dr. Daniella Kim (Research)

She will attack the method's defensibility and your sample. Answer with method and limits.

**F1 — "A use-error FMEA is a structured judgment exercise, not an empirical study. How do you defend the finding as evidence rather than as expert opinion?"** *(~85 words)*

> I don't defend it as an empirical finding — it isn't one, and that's the correct criticism. It's a structured hazard enumeration, and its validity rests on coverage and on the consequence ranking, not on a p-value. What made it evidentiary was that it was falsifiable in the rig: each predicted error mode was a testable prediction about where slips would occur, and the layout change either eliminated the observed critical inputs or it didn't. The analysis generated the hypotheses; the microgravity evaluation is what tested them.

**F2 — "How do you know the 30% and the error elimination aren't the same effect wearing two hats — a cleaner layout is faster and also less error-prone, so what did you actually learn?"** *(~80 words)*

> That's the right question, and I can't fully separate them with the design I had. What I can say is directional: the layout change was made for consequence-adjacency reasons, and the time reduction was not designed for. If they were one effect, I'd expect the time savings to be distributed across the task; the reason I believe they're separable is `[where the time actually came from]`. If I ran it now I'd hold layout constant and vary only adjacency, which is the study I'd want.

**F3 — "What would have changed your mind about the redesign?"** *(~60 words)*

> A critical input error surviving the layout change would have killed the adjacency hypothesis outright — that's the clean falsifier. The softer one: if error rate dropped only for the operators who were fastest, that tells me I bought the result with expertise rather than with structure, and structure is the only version that generalizes to a crew I haven't trained.

## NC04 — Follow-Ups: Systems / ML Engineering Lead

He wants requirements, thresholds, and observability. Do not give him a narrative.

**F1 — "Turn that into a requirement I could write an acceptance test against."** *(~80 words)*

> Two, and they're both testable at the hardware level. One: no control whose actuation is irreversible sits within the same reach envelope arc as a control actuated more than `[frequency threshold]` times per session — that's a geometry check against the CAD, no humans needed. Two: every irreversible control has a distinct actuation profile from its nearest neighbor, verified by a slip test under representative loading. The first is a design review gate; the second needs a rig.

**F2 — "Standards like MIL-STD-1472 are a floor. Where did that floor fail you, and what did you do about it?"** *(~85 words)*

> It fails at the interaction between requirements. 1472 and NASA-STD-3001 will each tell you a defensible thing about reach, force, and labeling, and a layout can satisfy both and still put a high-consequence control where a floating body naturally drifts. The standards are written per-element; the error mode is relational. So I treated compliance as a precondition and then ran the adjacency analysis on top of it. Clearing the standard was never the finding — it was the entry fee.

**F3 — "How would you instrument this so it's monitored in the field, not just validated once?"** *(~75 words)*

> Log near-misses, not errors. Errors are too rare to give you a signal before something bad happens. The observable I'd want is corrected actuations — an operator initiating an irreversible control and reversing within some window — because that's the same slip, caught. That gives you a rate you can trend per control, and a rising rate on a specific control is the earliest honest evidence that the layout stopped fitting how the system is actually being used.

## NC05 — Follow-Ups: Product Manager

She wants the decision it changed, at what cost, and whether you slow things down.

**F1 — "This sounds expensive and late. What did it actually cost the program, and would you do it again on a schedule?"** *(~80 words)*

> The expensive version is the one where you find it after layout freeze, because then it's tooling. Run consequence-ranked enumeration before freeze and it's an analysis, not a rebuild — that's precisely why I'd put the gate before the freeze rather than after. And I'd scope it: it applies only to irreversible controls, which is a small subset of any panel. I'm not asking for a full hazard analysis on every affordance, and if I did, you should say no.

**F2 — "Your recommendation made an action harder to take. When is that the wrong call?"** *(~70 words)*

> When the action is reversible, or when the timeline is short enough that delay is itself the hazard. That's the real tradeoff in air defense and I won't pretend it isn't. My rule is that friction has to be risk-proportional and it has to be paid for once — at authorization — not sprinkled across the interaction. If I can't name the specific error mode a piece of friction prevents, I shouldn't be arguing for it.

**F3 — "You were an intern. Why should I read this as your judgment?"** *(~65 words)*

> Because the method choice was the contribution, and that was mine — running it as a use-error analysis rather than the usability study that was the obvious move. Everything downstream followed from that call. I'd rather be judged on that decision than on my title at the time, and I'd point at the seven years since as evidence that it wasn't luck.

## NC06 — The Level Move: What You'd Own Today

**This is the answer that converts a good case study into a Lead/Staff case study.** It is not optional and it is not a follow-up — deliver beat [7] whether or not anyone asks.

Say it in the present tense, about today's scope, and make it something *other people execute without you*.

### Three phrasings — pick one, don't stack them

> **Gate version:** "The version of this I'd own now isn't the analysis, it's the gate: consequence-ranked use-error enumeration is a required artifact before layout freeze for any surface with an irreversible action, and it has an owner who isn't me."

> **Criterion version:** "What I'd leave behind is one acceptance criterion — no high-consequence control adjacent to a high-frequency one — because a criterion gets applied by whoever's designing at 2 a.m., and a finding gets forgotten by Friday."

> **Repository version:** "The reusable asset is the error-mode taxonomy, not the report. Once the modes are enumerated for a class of control surface, the next program inherits them and argues about the ranking instead of starting from a blank page."

### The sentence that must be said out loud

> "At NASA I produced the analysis. What I do now is produce the criterion — that's the difference between the work I did there and the work I'm applying for."

**Do not** claim you installed a gate at NASA. You didn't. The whole move depends on the tense being honest.

## NC07 — Cross-Track Map

The point of one case study is that it answers questions from every track. Learn which door each beat walks through.

| Beat | Also answers | How you pivot into it |
|---|---|---|
| [1] counterintuitive finding | Technical: falsifiability, surprising results | "The clearest example is the one where the result inverted on me —" |
| [4] method choice + rejected alternative | Technical: method selection, small-N defensibility, velocity vs. rigor | "I've had to pick between the rigorous study and the right one —" |
| [4] standards as floor | Technical: standards fluency · Systems: requirements | "MIL-STD-1472 and NASA-STD-3001 are where I learned what a standard can't tell you —" |
| [5] honest attribution | Behavioral: credibility, ownership · Culture: low-ego collaboration | "I'll separate what was mine from what was the team's —" |
| [6] speed isn't safety | Positioning: the Calibrated Cognitive Friction thesis | "This is where the friction argument actually comes from — it's not philosophical —" |
| [7] the gate | Behavioral: scope and influence · Positioning: the level argument | "The scope I'm arguing for is this: not the study, the criterion —" |
| [8] boundary of the analogy | Culture: intellectual honesty · Positioning: the no-defense-experience objection | "Here's where my experience stops being relevant —" |
| microgravity constraint | Behavioral: ambiguity, constrained environments · Design: physical-digital coherence | "The body wasn't braced, which breaks every assumption a terrestrial layout makes —" |

**Bridge sentence for a behavioral question when NASA is the only story you've got left:**

> "I'll use the NASA work again, but for a different reason this time — the part that's relevant here isn't the method, it's `[the specific beat]`."

Saying *why* you're reusing the story is what keeps it from reading as narrative hoarding. Reusing it silently costs you Relevance points.
