## Disagreeing with engineering (Alexa latency targets) — Tier 1

> **Question:** "Tell me about a time you disagreed with engineering." Or: *"Tell me about a conflict with a technical partner." "How do you win an argument you don't own?"*

### Answer

**Situation.** At Amazon, Alexa's latency targets were engineering guesses. Everyone agreed faster was better. Nobody could say how fast was fast enough, or whether it changed with what the user was doing.

**Task.** Replace the guesses with numbers from people, without trying to win on rank. They were engineering's targets, and I didn't own them.

**Action.** I didn't argue opinion against opinion. I proposed a measurement engineering could trust. I built a rig that set the delay on every trial, to the millisecond, across twelve interaction types. Then I wrote the result the way engineers write: a two-tier spec with pass criteria.

**Result.** Engineering adopted the thresholds as their latency targets, and teams still design against the spec. Alexa Economics' model projected about fifty million dollars over fifteen months.

**Earned secret.** The acceptable number depends on what the user is trying to do. One target for everything is too strict for some tasks and too loose for others.

**Mechanism.** The spec outlived the program. The disagreement ended because the data replaced both our opinions.

### Say this

- *"I replaced a guess with data, not with rank."*

## Conflict resolution — Tier 1

> **Question:** "Tell me about a conflict with a colleague and how you resolved it." Or: *"Tell me about a time a stakeholder was angry with you."*

**Situation.** At EchoStar, a lead designer and I clashed after I presented usability findings on their concept in a cross-functional review. They heard it as their work being taken apart in front of their partners, and the next review got tense.

## Working with designers and engineers — Tier 2

> **Question:** "How do you work with designers and engineers?" Or: *"How do you fit into the development process?"*

### Answer

**The claim.** I bring constraints, not verdicts. I arrive at concept review with the envelope: reach and anatomical limits, perceptual thresholds, and layout criteria.

**The decision it drives.** Human factors gets treated as a gate when it arrives late, and as a partner when it arrives early.

**Mechanism.** The hardware teams started pulling me into concept reviews by default, and the org-wide usability criteria I wrote are what they design against.

## Variant B: a human factors design problem — Tier 1

> **Question, hypothetical:** "Design the alerting for an inbound threat while the operator supervises multiple tracks." Or: *"Operators say the system is hard to use in the field. What do you do?"*

### Answer

**The claim.** I'm keeping this generic. The alert's job is to get the right track attended to in time, without teaching the operator to ignore alerts.

**How I'd know.** Start with situation awareness at three levels: does the operator perceive the track, understand it's a threat, and project where it's going? Then signal detection. Two things set the threshold: the asymmetry between the cost of a miss and the cost of a false alarm, and the base rate. When most tracks aren't threats, even a sensitive detector produces mostly false alarms. And every false alarm trains the operator to trust the next alarm less. So the top priority tier stays rare, and it's redundant across channels. The alert shows why the system flagged it, and a safe override is always one action away.

**Evidence, and where it stops.** I don't know your track density, alert philosophy, or operator workload. I'd ask, and I'd measure response under realistic time pressure, not in a quiet lab.

**The decision it drives.** Alert tiers with written criteria, a response-time target measured under load, and defined override behavior.

**What would change my mind.** If operators miss alerts because they're looking somewhere else, the fix is placement or channel, not threshold.

## Surveys and MaxDiff — Tier 2

> **Question:** "Have you run MaxDiff?" Or: *"How do you do quant with a small, hard-to-reach population?"*

### Answer

**The claim.** MaxDiff is best-worst scaling. Each screen shows a small set of items, and the respondent picks the most and least important. It forces trade-offs that rating scales let people dodge, when everything gets rated "very important."

**How I'd know.** A balanced design shows each item about equally often and alongside different items. Count analysis, best minus worst, gives a quick ranking. Hierarchical Bayes estimates individual-level scores by borrowing strength across respondents, and it needs each item shown several times per person. With a tiny operator population, I'd report counts with intervals and treat the ranking as directional.

**Evidence, and where it stops.** I haven't run one in production. Here's how I would. With a small operator population, I'd lead with counts and intervals, and treat the ranking as a prompt for interviews, not a final answer.

**The decision it drives.** A ranked list of what operators would trade away, and what they wouldn't.

### Say this

## Automation trust and calibrated friction — Tier 1

> **Question:** "How do you design for trust in autonomy?" Or: *"How do you keep operators from over-trusting the system?"*

### Answer

**The claim.** The goal is appropriate reliance. Operators accept correct recommendations fast and catch wrong ones. Over-trust and under-trust are both failures.

**How I'd know.** Lee and See, 2004, in *Human Factors*, frame trust as calibration to what the system can actually do. Parasuraman and Manzey, 2010, in *Human Factors*, show how complacency and automation bias show up under low-event-rate monitoring. Parasuraman, Sheridan, and Wickens, 2000, in *IEEE Transactions on Systems, Man, and Cybernetics*, give the levels of automation. So I measure reliance against system accuracy, not trust ratings alone.

**Evidence, and where it stops.** My Mercedes studies measured Level 2 and Level 3 handovers, and the guidelines improved control-transition safety and trust ratings. That's driving, not air defense. Same problem shape, different stakes.

**The decision it drives.** Friction goes only where an error is severe and irreversible. Everywhere else, remove it. The result is accuracy, fewer false engagements, and operator trust that drives adoption.

**What would change my mind.** If operators already catch wrong recommendations reliably, added friction just costs time, and I'd take it out.
