Crash course: the ideas behind the answers

How a defense program works

  • Human systems integration (HSI). Treating the people who operate and maintain a system as part of the system, planned with the same weight as hardware and software. DoD Instruction 5000.95 splits it into seven domains: human factors engineering, manpower, personnel, training, safety and occupational health, force protection and survivability, and habitability.
  • Manpower, personnel, and training (MPT). Three of those domains, as three questions: how many people does the system need, what kind of people with what skills, and what training gets them there.
  • MIL-STD-1472H. The military's design rulebook for the human side of equipment: control sizes, display legibility, reach distances, labels, and alarms. It says what good design looks like.
  • MIL-STD-46855A. The process rulebook. It says what human engineering work a contractor has to do, and when, across analysis, design, and test. 1472 is the "what"; 46855A is the "how and when."
  • MIL-STD-882E. The system safety standard. Each hazard is rated on how bad it would be and how likely it is, which gives its risk level. Fixes go in a fixed order: design the hazard out, add engineered safeguards, add warnings, and only then rely on procedures and training.
  • SAE6906A. The industry standard for running an HSI program. Each requirement is numbered so a program can pick which ones apply, which is called tailoring.
  • Data item description (DI-HFAC-…). The government's template for a document the contractor delivers, such as the HSI Program Plan.
  • Design reviews. The formal gates in a classic program: system requirements review, preliminary design review, critical design review, and test readiness review. Each one has human factors work due.
  • Interface control document (ICD). The agreement between two systems about exactly what data passes between them. If an operator sees that data, it needs human factors requirements, like how often it updates.
  • Human Readiness Level (HRL). A one-to-nine scale for whether a technology is ready for people to use safely, built to sit next to the Technology Readiness Level.

How a requirement is built

  • Requirement. A "shall" statement, plus a minimum acceptable value (the threshold), a goal value (the objective), the conditions it applies under, and how it will be checked.
  • Verification method. The four ways to prove a requirement is met: inspection (look at it), analysis (calculate it), demonstration (show it working), or test (measure it under controlled conditions).
  • Traceability. Each requirement links back to the analysis that produced it and forward to the test that proves it. Tools like Jama hold those links.

How I analyze the work

  • Task analysis. Breaking a job into its steps, decisions, and information needs. Almost everything else starts here.
  • Function allocation. Deciding who does each step, the human or the automation, and who has the authority to change that. Fitts lists, the old "humans are better at, machines are better at" tables, are only a starting point.
  • Usability FMEA. A failure modes and effects analysis for use errors: list every way a person could get a step wrong, rate how bad each would be, and fix the worst first.
  • Anthropometry. Body measurement. The usual rule sets reach for a small person, the fifth-percentile female, and clearance for a large one, the ninety-fifth-percentile male. ANSUR II is the Army's body-measurement survey.
  • Formative versus summative testing. Formative testing is small and repeated, and it finds problems to fix. Summative testing is larger and final, and it measures how well the design performs. Only summative results support an error rate or a safety claim.
  • Heuristic evaluation and cognitive walkthrough. Expert reviews without users. A heuristic evaluation checks the design against a list of rules, and a walkthrough steps through a task the way a new user would.
  • IMPRINT. Army software that simulates an operator's tasks step by step to predict workload and crew size before hardware exists.

How I measure the operator

  • Workload. How much of a person's mental capacity a task uses. The NASA Task Load Index, or NASA-TLX, is a six-question rating given after a task. The Bedford scale asks how much spare capacity was left.
  • Situation awareness (SA). Knowing what's going on, what it means, and what will happen next, which are Endsley's three levels. SAGAT pauses a simulation and quizzes the operator. SPAM asks questions while the task keeps running and times the answers.
  • Fatigue measures. The psychomotor vigilance task is a simple reaction-time test that catches lapses. The Karolinska Sleepiness Scale and the Samn-Perelli checklist are quick self-ratings.
  • Body-based measures. Functional near-infrared spectroscopy, or fNIRS, measures brain activity with light through the scalp. Eye tracking shows where people look, and heart-rate variability tracks sustained stress. They show that load changed, not why.
  • Psychophysics. Measuring the point at which people start to notice something, like a delay. That's how I set the latency thresholds.
  • Within-subject designs and mixed-effects models. In a within-subject design, each person tries every condition, which matters when operators are scarce. Mixed-effects models account for the fact that one person's many answers aren't independent.

How autonomy goes wrong, and how it's designed

  • Stages and levels of automation. Parasuraman, Sheridan, and Wickens split automation into four stages: gathering information, making sense of it, choosing an action, and carrying it out. Each stage can be automated to a different degree.
  • Adaptive versus adaptable automation. Adaptable means the operator changes the automation level. Adaptive means the system changes it on its own, so it has to announce the change.
  • Mode error and automation surprise. The operator believes the system is in one mode when it's in another, or the system does something the operator didn't expect.
  • Agent transparency. Chen and colleagues' model, from the Army Research Laboratory: show what the system is doing, why, and what it will do next, with how sure it is.
  • Trust calibration. People should rely on automation exactly as much as it deserves. Over-trust means accepting bad output, and disuse means ignoring good output.
  • Span of control and fan-out. How many vehicles one operator can supervise. Neglect time is how long a vehicle can run unattended, and interaction time is how long the operator needs to get it back on track. Fan-out is neglect time plus interaction time, divided by interaction time.
  • Signal detection. Every alert outcome is a hit, a miss, a false alarm, or a correct rejection. Too many false alarms teach operators to ignore the alarm.
  • Multiple resources. Wickens's idea that seeing and hearing, and words and spatial information, draw on partly separate mental capacity. That's why a critical cue goes on the channel the operator isn't already using.
  • Lost link. The control connection to an uncrewed vehicle drops. The operator should know in advance what each vehicle will do when that happens.

Follow-ups

Follow-up: "Walk me through 882."

MIL-STD-882E is the DoD's system safety standard. You rate each hazard on two scales. Severity has four categories: catastrophic, critical, marginal, and negligible. Probability has six levels: frequent, probable, occasional, remote, improbable, and eliminated. Together they give a risk level of high, serious, medium, or low, and the higher the risk, the more senior the person who has to formally accept it. Each hazard is tracked to closure in a hazard log. The fixes follow a set order: design the hazard out, reduce it through design changes, add engineered safeguards, add warning devices, and only then rely on signs, procedures, training, and protective equipment. My usability FMEA ran the same logic on use errors.

Follow-up: "Which human engineering standard do you work from?"

Two, because they do different jobs. MIL-STD-1472H is the design rulebook: what the controls, displays, and alarms should look like. MIL-STD-46855A is the process rulebook: what human engineering work the contractor does across analysis, design, and test. The old 1999 handbook was folded into that standard in 2011.

Follow-up: "Walk me through MIL-STD-46855A."

MIL-STD-46855A, from May 2011, sets out the human engineering work a contractor has to do in three phases: analysis, design and development, and test and evaluation. A 2016 notice confirmed it's still in use. The work gets delivered as documents built from government templates, such as the human engineering program plan and the test plan. The HSI Program Plan sits above all of that and covers all seven domains, but it doesn't replace those other plans unless the government asks it to.

Writing verifiable HF requirements — Tier 1

Question: "Write me an HF requirement for an autonomy status display that a test engineer can verify." Or: "How do you get HF into specs and ICDs?"

Evidence, and where it stops. At Amazon, the latency specification shipped as two tiers with pass-fail criteria for each type of interaction. I haven't written into a defense interface control document, but the logic is the same.

The decision it drives. Every human factors finding either becomes a requirement someone can trace and test, or it gets closed with a reason.

NASA: HSI, hazard analysis, and accommodation — Tier 1

Question: "Walk me through the NASA work." Or: "Tell me about your MIL-STD-1472 experience."

Answer

The claim. It was a human-systems integration evaluation of a Lunar Gateway medical workstation. It was formative, meaning the goal was to find and fix problems in a simulated setting, not to certify the design.

Setting a requirement from human data (latency) — Tier 1

Question: "How do you set a requirement when nobody knows the right number?" Or: "Tell me about your most impactful project."

Answer

The claim. Thresholds should come from people, measured under control, and they should ship as a requirement with its verification method and its limits.

How I'd know. I built a Wizard-of-Oz rig that set the response delay on every trial to the millisecond, and I used the method of constant stimuli: thirty participants rated twelve interaction types at six fixed delays, from half a second to three seconds, for about two thousand trials. They used a three-point slowness scale, because people notice slowness, not speed.

The requirement. Each interaction type got two tiers, read off the distribution of individual ratings, not the mean, with a psychometric fit across the delay levels. The high tier is the delay where more than seventy percent rate it not slow and fewer than five percent rate it too slow. The acceptable tier is more than half not slow and under fifteen percent too slow. Verification is pass-fail: you measure fielded latency against the band.

Evidence, and where it stops. Engineering adopted the thresholds as their targets, and an economics team's model projected about fifty million dollars. I held workload constant in that study. For operators supervising aircraft, I'd vary workload and fatigue on purpose.

The decision it drives. The output is a limit, a tolerance, and a pass-fail check that an engineer can run without me in the room.

Follow-ups

Follow-up: "Why constant stimuli, not a staircase?"

A staircase moves the delay up or down after each answer, and it homes in on a single point. I tested fixed delay levels instead, because I needed two cut-offs plus how quickly the ratings changed between them. And engineers have to trust the method before they'll trust the number.

Follow-up: "A voice assistant isn't an aircraft."

Agreed, and the numbers don't carry over. The method does: when a person depends on an automated response, the timing limit should come from measuring people under controlled conditions. Here, I'd add the workload I held constant.

Formative testing with small samples — Tier 2

Question: "How many participants do you need?" Or: "Isn't five people too few?"

Answer

The claim. Early-stage testing works best small and repeated. I typically run about five people per round for about three rounds, and I change the design between rounds.

How I'd know. At that stage, the goal is to find mistakes people make and rank them by how serious they are, not to estimate how often they happen. Three small rounds find more problems than one big round, because each round tests a design that already fixed the last round's issues.

Where it stops. A small-round finding justifies a design decision, but it doesn't support a safety claim or an error rate. If someone needs a rate, that's a larger final study, called a summative study, and I'd scope it that way.

Heuristic analysis and cognitive walkthroughs — Tier 2

Question: "How would you evaluate a prototype hardware and software interface?"

Answer

The heuristic analysis. That's an expert review against a list of rules. I build the list from MIL-STD-1472H plus rules specific to the task: can the operator always see which mode the system is in, are the alerts easy to tell apart, does input work with gloves and gear on, and can the screen be read at a distance and at night. Two reviewers work separately and then compare.

The cognitive walkthrough. I step through each critical task the way a new operator would. At each step, I ask whether they'd know what to do, whether they'd see how to do it, and whether the feedback would tell them it worked.

The output. Each finding gets rated on the same severity scale as the hazard analysis and tied to the rule it breaks, and the top items get written as requirements.

Where it stops. Expert reviews find rule violations, but they don't show how real operators will perform. The serious findings get confirmed in a test with real operators in the loop.

Span of control and multi-aircraft supervision — Tier 1

Question: "How many aircraft can one operator supervise, and how would you find out?"

Answer

The claim. The operator-to-aircraft ratio is a capacity number that you measure, not one that you choose.

How I'd know. Olsen and Wood frame it with two times. Neglect time is how long an aircraft can run on its own before things start to go wrong, and interaction time is how long the operator needs to get it back on track. Fan-out, the number of aircraft one person can handle, is neglect time plus interaction time, divided by interaction time. A 2025 robotics paper found that formula overestimates, because the real number depends on how well the operator judges which aircraft can be left alone. That makes span of control a trust-calibration problem, not just an attention problem. So I'd ramp the number of aircraft in a simulation with seeded events, and I'd track time to detect, error catch rate, and workload.

My hypothesis. Capacity tops out where checking breaks down. Past a certain point, operators stop checking the automation's work and start just accepting it, and that point is a number I can put in a requirement.

The decision it drives. The output is a span-of-control requirement with the conditions it holds under. When one operator hits their limit, the alternative to pushing the ratio higher is having several operators share the fleet.

Say this

  • "Span of control is the point where operators stop checking."

Function allocation — Tier 1

Question: "Walk me through function allocation for a multi-aircraft mission."

Answer

The claim. Fitts lists, the old tables of what humans do better and what machines do better, are a starting point. Real allocation decides, for each stage of the work, how much the automation does, who holds authority at each moment, and what triggers a handoff.

How I'd know. For each mission phase, from planning to tasking to monitoring to stepping in, I decide who handles the four stages: gathering information, making sense of it, choosing an action, and carrying it out. The level can shift during the mission, triggered by measured workload, fatigue, the mission phase, or a critical event. If the operator makes that shift, it's called adaptable automation. If the system makes it on its own, it's adaptive, and the system has to announce the change.

Across vehicles. For third-party platforms brought into Lattice, the way authority and modes are shown should stay the same from one vehicle to the next.

Evidence, and where it stops. The Mercedes work was about handing control back to a driver who wasn't expecting it, and my patent work is about mode error, when the system is in a different mode than the user thinks. Neither was military flight.

The decision it drives. The output is a table of who does what, who has authority, and the rules for handing off, and each handoff becomes a requirement.

Say this

  • "Every handoff of authority is a clear, visible change."

Third-party platforms in Lattice — Tier 2

How I'd verify it. I'd run situation awareness probes on mixed fleets, and I'd check whether operators predict each vehicle's behavior as well as they do with a single platform.

Human-autonomy transparency — Tier 1

Question: "What does the operator need to see about what the autonomy is doing, and why?"

Answer

The claim. The operator needs to see what the autonomy is doing, why it's doing it, and what it will do next, along with how sure it is.

How I'd know. That's the situation-awareness-based agent transparency model from Chen and colleagues at the Army Research Laboratory, which has those three levels. The closest public example is IMPACT, a research system for managing a mixed group of robotic vehicles. The failure to design against is automation surprise, where the system changes mode and the operator doesn't notice. My patent was on controls where one input means different things in different modes, which is exactly where those mode errors come from.

The evidence on cost. Studies built on that model show operators performing better and trusting more appropriately as the system shows more of its reasoning. One study, Selkowitz and colleagues in 2015, found that showing more helped people trust the system the right amount without adding much workload, but it didn't improve their awareness of the situation. So I'd test how much to show under realistic workload, rather than assume it costs attention.

The decision it drives. The outputs are requirements for mode annunciation, intent display, and showing confidence separately from data freshness, verified with situation awareness probes and response times to automation surprises.

Say this

  • "No mode change the operator didn't see."

Workload and situation awareness — Tier 1

Question: "How do you measure workload and situation awareness?"

Answer

The claim. I manipulate load deliberately, and I pair subjective, performance, and physiological measures.

How I'd know. At Amazon, I co-established a cognitive load framework with fNIRS and eye tracking, and at Mercedes, I measured situation awareness and reaction time during takeovers. On this program, I'd add the NASA-TLX after each block and the Bedford scale for spare capacity. For situation awareness, I'd use SAGAT freeze probes when I need precision, and SPAM real-time probes when the task can't stop.

Evidence, and where it stops. Physiology tells you that load changed, not why, and I never claim it beat the NASA-TLX. I haven't run SAGAT or SPAM on a program, so I'd pilot the probe set before a test event.

The decision it drives. The output is a baseline for each phase of the mission, and limits that requirements can point to.

Fatigue across a long mission — Tier 1

Question: "How would you measure fatigue across a long mission and turn it into a requirement?"

Answer

The claim. Fatigue builds with time on task and depends on time of day, so I measure it across the whole length of a mission and turn the drop in performance into design rules.

How I'd know. I'd use the psychomotor vigilance task, a simple reaction-time test that catches lapses, plus two quick self-ratings, the Karolinska Sleepiness Scale and the Samn-Perelli checklist, at fixed intervals. I'd also track performance on the real tasks. That takes long simulation runs, not short sessions.

Evidence, and where it stops. My dissertation was on memory and reaction time when people are interrupted under high stress. Fatigue in the lab isn't the same as fatigue in operations, so I'd confirm the findings at test events.

The decision it drives. The outputs are requirements for alerts designed for a tired operator late in the mission, schedules that don't pile critical tasks at the end, and handoff rules that limit how long one person supervises, each tested over a full-length mission.

  • "Design the alert for the operator at hour six, not hour one."

Automation trust with skeptical operators — Tier 1

Question: "Pilots are skeptical of autonomy. How do you design for, and measure, appropriate trust?"

Answer

The claim. I'm after the right amount of reliance. Trusting a system too much and refusing to use it are both failures, and with skeptical operators, not using it may be the bigger risk.

How I'd know. Lee and See describe good trust as matching what the system can actually do. Parasuraman and Manzey showed that people get complacent when they're juggling several tasks, which is exactly what supervising several aircraft is. So I measure reliance against reliability. Over-trust shows up as acceptance staying flat across confidence bands, and disuse shows up as overrides of correct recommendations.

Evidence, and where it stops. At Mercedes, I studied the alerts that hand control back to a driver. Those participants were drivers, not pilots.

The decision it drives. I'd log reliance and recheck it after every model update. Trust gets earned by the system behaving predictably, with the operators themselves taking part in the test events.

Say this

  • "Trust is earned by predictability, and it's measured against reliability."

Degraded conditions and lost link — Tier 1

Question: "The link degrades or drops mid-mission. How does the operator know, and how do they recover?"

Answer

The claim. The operator has to know when the system is degraded, not just when it's wrong, and the design has to keep working in that state.

How I'd know. I'd build the scenarios from field reports and subject-matter experts. They'd cover a lost or slow connection, navigation without GPS, failing sensors, weather and night, changes in autonomy mode, fatigue late in a mission, and handoffs between operators or sites. Then I'd measure whether the operator notices the problem, how long recovery takes, and the mistakes made along the way.

Two rules. First, old data can still produce a confident-looking recommendation, so the display has to show confidence and data freshness separately. Second, acknowledgment and completion are different signals; collapse them and operators double-actuate. And if the connection drops, the operator should already know what each aircraft will do.

The decision it drives. The outputs are requirements for showing when the system is degraded and for what it does when it falls back, plus the scenarios that belong in training.

Say this

  • "Separate confidence from freshness, and acknowledgment from completion."

Requirement template. A "shall" statement, a minimum and a goal value, the conditions, and how it's checked: inspection, analysis, demonstration, or test.

Fan-out. (Neglect time + interaction time) ÷ interaction time. Real capacity is lower, because it depends on how well the operator judges which aircraft can be left alone.

882. How bad times how likely gives the risk. Fix order: design it out, design change, safeguards, warnings, and only then signs, procedures, training, and protective equipment.
