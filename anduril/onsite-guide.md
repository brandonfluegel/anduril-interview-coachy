## Mission conviction — Tier 1

> **Question:** "Are you comfortable working on systems that engage targets?" Or: *"How do you feel about working on weapons?"*

### Answer

**The answer.** Yes, and I'll say that directly. These systems protect service members, and I want to help build them. It's a big part of why I applied.

**The reason.** When the system has to act in seconds, the human decision is the part most likely to fail and the part that matters most. Human factors is how you get the right engagement decision and avoid the wrong one, whether that's fratricide or a false engagement.

**What I bring.** I help build systems where operators can see why the system flagged something, predict what it will do, and override it safely. That's what makes an operator fast when the system is right, and able to catch it when it's wrong.

**The decision it drives.** Where my work shows up is accuracy and trust. If operators don't trust the system, they work around it, and then it isn't protecting anyone.

> ⚠️ **Never use the research report's phrasing in this room.** No "moral crumple zone," and no "make harm easier to authorize."

### Follow-ups

**Follow-up: "Where should the human sit in the loop?" Or: "How do you think about autonomy and human control?"**

> DoD Directive 3000.09, updated January 25, 2023, frames it as appropriate levels of human judgment over the use of force. I don't think of it as a single dial. Parasuraman, Sheridan, and Wickens split automation into four stages: acquiring information, analyzing it, selecting a decision, and taking action. Map those onto detect, track, identify, and defeat, and each stage can carry a different level of automation. Detection and tracking can be highly automated. Identification is where the operator needs to see why the system thinks what it thinks. And the decision to defeat is where human judgment has to be meaningful at the speed the system actually runs. Exactly where the human sits at each stage is something I'd want to decide with operator data rather than assume.

> ⚠️ **Keep it operational and anchored in policy.** No ethics-report phrasing.

### Answer

**The hook.** Today I'll show you two projects with the same shape. Eighty percent of the work is rapid field and qualitative research. Twenty percent is the quantitative and human factors layer that makes each requirement defensible, and on Echo Hub, I'll show you what that layer was worth to engineering. Then I'll close with what I've been building since, and what my first ninety days here would look like. I'll go deep on method and thresholds, and keep internal figures and competitive benchmarking out of it.

**Echo Hub, the eighty percent.** I ran in-home contextual inquiry in about a dozen households in Seattle and Denver. People praised the panel, then reached for their phones. That compensating behavior became the shall statement: the device shall resolve to the controls relevant to that room and time of day, before the user reaches it. It's verified by approach-distance testing against the glance-legibility criterion.

**Echo Hub, getting it built with RIDE.** A finding like that doesn't help anyone sitting in a report, so I didn't write one and walk away. I'd introduced RIDE, Rapid Iterative Design and Evaluation, as the way we ran design on the Echo portfolio. It's short test-fix-retest cycles with design and engineering: we test a build with a handful of people, fix what breaks, and retest within days, before development commits. That kept the research inside the design cycle instead of trailing behind it.

**Echo Hub, the twenty percent.** Echo Hub is an Alexa device, and Alexa interactions are multimodal. You speak or tap, and the response can come back as speech, a screen update, or something happening in the home. Whether that feels responsive decides whether people trust it or reach for their phone instead. So alongside glance-first criteria for legibility and information density at walking distance, the quantitative layer underneath was the Alexa latency work.

**Echo Hub, the latency problem.** When I started, Alexa's latency targets were engineering guesses, basically one number for everything. That meant engineering was spending effort making some responses faster than anyone could notice, while others still weren't fast enough.

**Echo Hub, the latency method.** So I set the thresholds from people instead, using psychophysics. I built a Wizard-of-Oz rig that controlled the delay on every trial to the millisecond: thirty participants, twelve interaction types, and six delay levels from half a second to three seconds, for about two thousand trials. People rated how slow each response felt.

**Echo Hub, what the latency work found.** The acceptable delay depended on what the person was trying to do. Interactions that stand in for a physical action needed to come back in about half a second. For conversational responses, coming back too fast felt wrong too, so the spec needed a lower bound as well as an upper one. The output was a two-tier spec with pass criteria for each interaction type, and engineering adopted it as their targets.

**Echo Hub, the return.** The value to engineering was that they could stop chasing speed nobody could perceive and put the effort where delay actually hurt. An economics team's model projected about fifty million dollars over fifteen months from that work. I'm careful to call it a projection from their model. What I own is the thresholds and the spec. I also got a company metric renamed, because its name assumed the measured delay and the felt delay were the same thing. Both outlived me.

**Echo Hub, the transfer.** Both halves carry over. The field work is the same problem as a display that several roles glance at from a distance while they're doing something else. The latency work is about a person waiting on an AI system to respond, and that timing requirement should come from people, measured under control. I'd bring the method, not the numbers, and since I held workload constant in that study, here I'd make operator load part of it.

**NASA, the eighty percent.** I ran contextual inquiry and task observation with subject-matter experts. That gave me the task list, and then a service blueprint of the clinical workflows.

**NASA, the twenty percent.** I ran a usability FMEA that severity-ranked three to four medical scenarios. Reach and clearance came from anthropometric data, with reach set to the fifth-percentile female and clearance to the ninety-fifth-percentile male, against NASA-STD-3001 and MIL-STD-1472.

**NASA, the result.** The worst failure modes came from adjacent controls with different severity consequences, and we separated them over three to four iterations. The final round had no critical input errors, and task time fell about thirty percent as a side effect.

**The EchoStar coda, two minutes.** Since June 2025, I've built the human factors function at EchoStar. On one hardware program, I brought the reach envelope into the concept review instead of the design review, and a control placement moved before any tooling existed. That was cheap then, and it would have been expensive three months later. I also brought RIDE over from Amazon. It's the same habit as Echo Hub, just more recent: get the human factors input in while the change is still cheap.

## When you got it wrong (Echo Show benchmark) — Tier 1

> **Question:** "Tell me about a failure." Or: *"Tell me about a study that didn't go as planned." "When has your research been wrong?"*

### Answer

**Situation.** My first project at Amazon was a benchmark of Echo Show against competitors on high-priority tasks. Nobody commissioned it. I recruited participants myself and spent two weeks on it.

**Result.** At the readout, the director of design asked who had asked for it, and I had no answer. The roadmap was already set, and the work changed nothing.

**Earned secret.** The lesson was that initiative doesn't count for much if nobody agreed up front what the result would change. The study was solid, and the finding was real. Rigor wasn't the problem.

**Mechanism.** Now, before any study runs, stakeholders commit in writing to what each possible result will change. If every branch leads to the same decision, the study doesn't run. That rule has killed proposals of mine.

**Transfer.** On a team that ships fast, that rule is what keeps research from feeling like a tax, because every study is tied to a real decision and a date.

**Situation.** At Amazon, Alexa's latency targets were engineering guesses. Everyone agreed faster was better, but nobody could say how fast was fast enough, or whether it changed with what the user was doing.

**Mechanism.** The spec outlived the program. And once there was a test to pass, neither of us was arguing from opinion anymore.

## Research ops and repositories — Tier 1

> **Question:** "How would you uplevel research operations?" Or: *"How do you make insights reusable?"*

# Part 5 · Field research with operators

> **The through-line.** What people do matters more than what they say. I watch the work where it happens, use groups only to test what I think I saw, map the whole service rather than the screen, test small and often, and rank what fails by severity.

## Contextual inquiry and rapid field ethnography — Tier 2

> **Question:** "How do you run contextual inquiry in the field?" Or: *"How do you do rapid ethnography?"*

### Answer

**The claim.** I work from four principles: context, partnership, interpretation, and focus. I go where the work happens, the operator is the expert, I check my interpretation with them on the spot, and every session has a focus tied to a decision.

**How I'd know.** Ride-alongs are my strongest tool, because I see the work under real conditions instead of hearing a description of it. At Uber in Brazil, I rode along with drivers, working through a translator. In a setting like that, my job is to be the least disruptive person in the room, so people work the way they normally would.

**Where it stops.** Observation shows me what happens, but not how often it happens. So I treat it as a source of hypotheses, and I use formative testing to check them.

**The decision it drives.** And I earn the next session by coming back and showing what changed because of the last one. In my experience, that's the best way to keep getting time with operators.

## Focus groups and group methods with operators — Tier 2

> **Question:** "How would you run focus groups with operators?" Or: *"When do you use group methods?"*

### Answer

**The claim.** I use groups for reactions to concepts and for testing a synthesis. I don't use them to learn what people actually do, because in a group, what people say they do drifts toward what sounds right.

## Usability testing with small n — Tier 2

> **Question:** "How many participants do you need?" Or: *"Isn't five people too few?"*

### Answer

**The claim.** Formative testing works best small and repeated: about five people per round, for about three rounds, with the design changing between rounds.

**How I'd know.** The goal at that stage is to find use errors and rank them by severity, not to estimate how often they happen. A small sample run several times finds more problems than one large sample run once, because each round tests a design that has already fixed the last round's issues.

**Where it stops.** The limit is what that evidence can claim. A formative finding is enough to justify a design decision, but not a safety claim. If someone needs an error rate, that's a different study with a different sample size, and I'd scope it that way.

## Usability FMEA and use-error analysis — Tier 2

> **Question:** "Walk me through a use-error analysis." Or: *"What's your usability FMEA experience?"*

### Answer

**The claim.** I work it from both directions: top-down from the harms, and bottom-up, step by step. The places where the two don't line up are usually where the problems are.

**How I'd know.** For each step, I ask whether the operator can perceive, understand, and act correctly. A failure at any of the three is a use-error mode, and I rank it by severity. Detectability means whether the operator or system can catch the error before the consequence, not whether a lab test catches it.

**Evidence, and where it stops.** At NASA, the worst failure modes came from adjacent controls with very different severity consequences, so a slip of the hand could trigger the wrong action. We fixed that in the layout itself rather than adding a warning. I'm careful to describe it as formative work on a small sample.

**The decision it drives.** The output is a critical task list, with every control traced back to the scenario it protects against, so anyone can see why each design decision was made.

# Part 6 · Turning human data into numbers

> **The through-line.** A requirement needs a number, and the number has to come from people, measured with control, with its limits said out loud. Quantitative work is the twenty percent that makes the eighty percent defensible.

## Setting a number from human data (latency psychophysics) — Tier 1

> **Question:** "How do you set a requirement when nobody knows the right number?" Or: *"Tell me about your most impactful project."*

### Answer

**The claim.** My view is that thresholds should come from people, measured with real experimental control. A real system's latency varies from moment to moment, so you can't pin down the threshold just by watching the product.

**How I'd know.** I built a Wizard-of-Oz rig that set the response delay on every trial, to the millisecond, across twelve interaction types. It looked and sounded real to the participant, but the timing was mine. That let me find, for each kind of request, where delay started to matter.

**Evidence, and where it stops.** The spec had two tiers with pass criteria, and it was intent-dependent, because the data was. An economics team's model projected about fifty million dollars over fifteen months. The limit I named is that workload was held constant. Here, I'd manipulate operator load, because a threshold that holds at rest may not hold under pressure.

**The decision it drives.** The output is a threshold, a tolerance, and a pass criterion an engineer can test against without me in the room.

**What would change my mind.** If the threshold shifts a lot with load, the spec would need separate numbers for different load conditions.

### Follow-ups

**Follow-up: "Did your work produce that fifty million?"**

> That's a projection from an economics team's model. I didn't measure it. What I own is the thresholds and the spec, and I always call it "projected."

**Follow-up: "What was the dependent measure, what procedure, and how did you pick the number?"**

> The dependent measure was a unipolar three-point slowness scale rather than a bipolar fast–slow scale, because people notice slowness, not speed. It was a within-subjects design: each of thirty participants saw six fixed delay levels, from half a second to three seconds, across the twelve interaction types, for about two thousand trials. So it was a method of constant stimuli rather than a staircase. The two tiers are two pass levels per interaction type, read off that rating curve. I set each pass level using the distribution of individual ratings instead of the average, because if you build a spec on the average, you end up designing for the median user and missing everyone at the edges. Every threshold shipped with four things: the line, its confidence interval, the slope of the curve there, and the mode and workload it applies to. Engineering built to the cautious end of the interval. The high tier is the delay where more than seventy percent rate it not slow and fewer than five percent rate it too slow. The acceptable tier is more than half not slow and under fifteen percent too slow.

**Follow-up: "Why constant stimuli, not a staircase?"**

> A staircase finds one point. I needed two lines plus the slope, and it's harder for engineers to accept.

**Follow-up: "How was workload held constant?"**

> People made the judgments during a light visual monitoring task at a fixed difficulty, set from a practice block. I ran NASA-TLX after each block as a manipulation check only, to confirm the load landed where I aimed it, not as a result.

> ⚠️ **The dependent measure must match every other description of this program.** If they saw the presentation, point back and go straight to the procedure and the cutoff.

## Surveys and MaxDiff — Tier 2

> **Question:** "Have you run MaxDiff?" Or: *"How do you do quant with a small, hard-to-reach population?"*

### Answer

**The claim.** MaxDiff is best-worst scaling. Each screen shows a small set of items, and the respondent picks the most and least important. It forces the trade-offs people get to avoid on a rating scale, where everything ends up "very important."

**How I'd know.** A balanced design shows each item about equally often, and alongside different items. Count analysis, best minus worst, gives a quick ranking. Hierarchical Bayes estimates individual-level scores by borrowing strength across respondents, and it needs each item shown several times per person. With a tiny operator population, I'd report counts with intervals and treat the ranking as directional.

**Evidence, and where it stops.** I haven't run one in production, so I'm describing how I'd approach it. With a small operator population, I'd lead with counts and intervals, and use the ranking to guide follow-up interviews rather than treat it as the final answer.

**The decision it drives.** The output is a ranked list of what operators would trade away, and what they wouldn't.

## Measuring workload in the field — Tier 2

> **Question:** "How do you measure workload with operators?" Or: *"What do biometrics add?"*

### Answer

**The claim.** I start with the cheapest measure that answers the question, which usually means NASA-TLX and task performance. Eye tracking shows what operators didn't notice, because people can't report what they never saw. Heart-rate variability or skin conductance tracks load over long periods.

**How I'd know.** I co-established a cognitive load framework with fNIRS and eye tracking at Amazon. fNIRS is worth it only when the question really needs a continuous, objective measure of load, and in the field it's usually more than you need.

**Where it stops.** Physiology can tell you that load changed, but it can't tell you why. You need the task data and the operator's own account alongside it.

**The decision it drives.** I pair physiological measures with error data and deliberately manipulate workload, so I'm never resting a conclusion on one signal.

## Situation awareness — Tier 2

### Answer

## Automation trust and calibrated friction — Tier 1

> **Question:** "How do you design for trust in autonomy?" Or: *"How do you keep operators from over-trusting the system?"*

### Answer

**The claim.** What I'm after is appropriate reliance: operators accept correct recommendations quickly and catch the wrong ones. Over-trust and under-trust are both problems.

**How I'd know.** Lee and See, 2004, in *Human Factors*, frame trust as calibration to what the system can actually do. Parasuraman and Manzey, 2010, in *Human Factors*, show that complacency and automation bias show up when operators are juggling several tasks at once, rather than in simple single-task monitoring. Parasuraman, Sheridan, and Wickens, 2000, in *IEEE Transactions on Systems, Man, and Cybernetics*, give the levels of automation. So I measure reliance against how accurate the system actually is, instead of relying on trust ratings alone. Over-trust shows up as acceptance rate going flat across the system's confidence bands, and decision time collapsing on low-confidence items.

**Evidence, and where it stops.** My Mercedes studies measured Level 2 and Level 3 handovers, and the guidelines improved control-transition safety and trust ratings. That was driving, and air defense is obviously a much higher-stakes setting, but a lot of the same dynamics apply.

**The decision it drives.** I'd put friction where a false engagement would cost more than the delay, which depends on classification confidence and time-to-impact. What you get is better accuracy, fewer false engagements, and operators who trust the system enough to use it.

**What would change my mind.** If operators already catch wrong recommendations reliably, added friction just costs time, and I'd take it out.

## I read your research response — Tier 1

> **Question:** "I read your research response from the conference." Or: *"You wrote about adding friction. Doesn't that slow operators down?"*

### Answer

**The frame.** That piece was a sketch, written quickly in response to questions posed at the conference. I've kept thinking about it, and my view changed once I applied it to counter-UAS.

**What changed.** A rule like "friction only where an error is severe and irreversible" doesn't hold up in air defense, because a missed threat is severe and irreversible too. If you gate an engagement, you can end up trading one irreversible error for another.

**The rule now.** Where I've landed is that friction belongs where a false engagement would cost more than the delay, based on classification confidence and time-to-impact.

**How it plays out.** With high confidence and a short time-to-impact, the system should automate and keep the operator informed. With an ambiguous identification and time to spare, it should ask for confirmation. The hard cases are in between, and that's where I'd focus the research.

**What would change my mind.** What I'd really want is operator data: how often ambiguous identifications actually come up, and how long the decision takes under load.

## Researching degraded conditions — Tier 1

> **Question:** "How would you research degraded conditions?" Or: *"What happens when things go wrong in the field?" "How do you test off-nominal?"*

> ⚠️ **I asked the principal designer about degraded conditions in my screen.** Expect it back.

### Answer

**The claim.** The normal case is the easy part. Operators need to know when the system is degraded, as well as when it's wrong, and the design has to keep working in that degraded state.

**How I'd know.** I build the degraded scenarios from field reports and subject-matter experts first. I'd cover comms loss or lag; sensor dropout or conflicting tracks; night, weather, and glare; gloves, cold, and noise; fatigue late in a shift; workload spikes; and handoffs in the middle of an event. Then I'd run formative testing with those degradations injected, and measure three things: whether the operator detects the degraded state, how long recovery takes, and the errors made during it. A stale input can produce a high-confidence recommendation, so the display has to separate confidence from input freshness. And acknowledgment and completion are two signals. Collapse them and operators double-actuate.

**Evidence, and where it stops.** At Mercedes, I ran handovers where automation handed control back to the driver under surprise, which is degraded operation by design, and I measured situation awareness and reaction time through it. I haven't tested in military field conditions, so I'd want the field team's list of what actually fails.

**The decision it drives.** The output is design inputs for degraded-mode indication, defined fallback behavior, and the scenarios that belong in training.

**What would change my mind.** If operators already pick up on degradation reliably, I'd shift the focus from indicating it to recovering from it: how quickly and safely they get back to a working state.

### Say this

- *"Operators need to know when the system is degraded, and not only when it's wrong."*

## Team and crew research — Tier 2

> **Question:** "Air defense is multi-role. How would you research crews, not just individual operators?" Or: *"How do you study handoffs between operators?"*

### Answer

**The claim.** In a multi-role system, I'd study the crew as a whole. Each role needs the situation awareness its own job requires, and the crew needs shared awareness wherever those jobs overlap.

**How I'd know.** I'd observe crews during exercises and test events, and focus on handoffs, shift changes, and the moments when one role's information has to reach another. I'd analyze communications: who talks to whom, when, and how often someone has to ask for something to be repeated or clarified, because those requests show where shared awareness is breaking down. And I'd look at shared displays: what the common picture shows everyone, and what each role needs on top of it.

**Evidence, and where it stops.** Echo Hub was a shared display used by several people with different intents, and my service blueprints map handoffs as their own lane. I haven't run a formal team situation awareness study with a military crew, and I'd be upfront about that.

**The decision it drives.** The output is handoff requirements, a split between the common picture and role-specific views, and a list of communication breakdowns to design out.

**What would change my mind.** If breakdowns cluster at shift changes rather than mid-event, I'd look at procedure and briefing before changing the display.

## Reference cards

[[card]] **Signal detection theory.** There are four outcomes: a hit, a miss, a false alarm, and a correct rejection. Sensitivity is how well the signal separates from the noise, and the criterion is where you set the threshold. Payoffs and base rate together set the right criterion. With too many false alarms, operators learn to ignore the alarm.

[[card]] **Endsley's levels of situation awareness.** Level one is perceiving what's there, level two is comprehending what it means, and level three is projecting what happens next.

[[card]] **Multiple resource theory.** Visual and auditory input, spatial and verbal codes, and perception and response each draw on separate resources. Two tasks on the same resource interfere with each other more than two tasks on different resources.

[[card]] **Levels of automation, mapped to DTID.** Parasuraman, Sheridan, and Wickens split automation into four stages: information acquisition, information analysis, decision selection, and action implementation. Acquisition maps to detect and track, analysis maps to identify, and decision and action map to defeat. Each stage can sit at a different level of automation, so "where the human sits" has four answers rather than one.

[[card]] **The counter-UAS sequence.** The standard shorthand is detect, track, identify, and defeat, or DTID. The longer kill-chain version, F2T2EA, runs find, fix, track, target, engage, and assess. I use DTID.

[[card]] **Anduril, publicly announced.** The posting names Lattice OS, "an AI-powered operating system that turns thousands of data streams into a real-time, 3D command and control center." Anduril.com describes Sentry as an autonomous surveillance platform that detects, identifies, and tracks objects of interest; Anvil as an autonomous interceptor that gives visual feedback for positive identification by a human operator; Roadrunner as an operator-supervised autonomous air vehicle, with Roadrunner-M as its interceptor variant for ground-based air defense; and Pulsar as a family of software-defined electromagnetic warfare systems.

> ⚠️ **Cite these as public announcements, and ask how they were designed.** Never assert how the team built them.

## Also in this theme

**Choosing visual, audio, or haptic — Tier 3.** I pick the channel based on what the operator is already using, which comes straight from multiple resource theory. Critical information gets redundancy across channels, with defined behavior when channels conflict or one drops out.

**The patent — Tier 3.** My patent is on context-dependent control mapping, where one physical actuation produces different commands depending on context. A mode error happens when what's actually active doesn't match what the user thinks is active.

**Principles for Agentic Trust (only if asked) — Tier 3.** The framework was accepted at CSCW 2026 Industry Perspectives. ## Variant A: stand up a research plan — Tier 1

> **Question, hypothetical:** "A new operator-facing capability ships in eight weeks. Operator access is limited. Plan the research."

### Answer

**The claim.** I'm treating this as a hypothetical. How much we need to be able to claim sets the method. Eight weeks and limited access gets you directional evidence and a severity-ranked list of use errors, but not a validated rate.

## Variant B: a human factors design problem — Tier 1

> **Question, hypothetical:** "Design the alerting for an inbound threat while the operator supervises multiple tracks." Or: *"Operators say the system is hard to use in the field. What do you do?"*

### Answer

**The claim.** I'll keep this generic. The alert has to get the operator looking at the right track in time, without training them to tune alerts out.

**How I'd know.** I start with situation awareness at three levels: does the operator perceive the track, understand that it's a threat, and project where it's going? Then signal detection. Two things set the threshold: the asymmetry between the cost of a miss and the cost of a false alarm, and the base rate. When most tracks aren't threats, even a sensitive detector produces mostly false alarms, and every false alarm trains the operator to trust the next alarm less. So the top priority tier stays rare, and it's redundant across channels. The alert shows why the system flagged it, and a safe override is always one action away.

**Evidence, and where it stops.** I don't know your track density, your alert philosophy, or your operators' workload. I'd ask, and I'd measure response under realistic time pressure rather than in a quiet lab.

**The decision it drives.** The output is alert tiers with written criteria, a response-time target measured under load, and defined override behavior.

**What would change my mind.** If operators miss alerts because they're looking somewhere else, I'd change the placement or the channel rather than the threshold.

## If they hand you a scenario or a system — Tier 2

I say these six steps out loud before starting, because interviewers want to hear how I'm approaching it.

- First, I pin down the operators, the mission phase, and the environment.
- Second, I break the task into steps small enough that each one can fail.
- Third, for each step, I ask whether the operator can perceive, understand, and act correctly. I look for omission, wrong sequence, wrong object, and wrong timing.
- Fourth, I mark as critical any task where an error could cause serious harm, like a missed threat or fratricide. I decide that by severity, whatever the likelihood.
- Fifth, I order the controls: design the error out, protect against it, and inform the operator last.
- And sixth, I define the formative test that shows each control works.

## BLUF readout — Tier 1

> **Question:** "How do you communicate findings?" Or: *"Give me a readout."*

### Answer

**The claim.** I use bottom line up front: the finding, its impact on the product or mission, the recommended change, and my confidence.
