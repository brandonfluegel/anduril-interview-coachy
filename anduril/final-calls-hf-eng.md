## Writing verifiable HF requirements — Tier 1 · NEW

> **Question:** "Write me an HF requirement for an autonomy status display that a test engineer can verify." Or: *"How do you get HF into specs and ICDs?"*

### Answer

**The claim.** A requirement is a shall statement, a measurable criterion with threshold and objective values, the conditions, and a verification method: inspection, analysis, demonstration, or test.

**The sample.** "The control station shall display the active autonomy mode for each aircraft under control. Threshold: trained operators identify the active mode within two seconds on at least ninety-five percent of probes, during a representative multi-aircraft scenario. Objective: one second, ninety-nine percent. Verification: test, in a human-in-the-loop evaluation." The numbers are illustrative. The real ones come from data.

**Traceability.** In Jama, each requirement links to the analysis it came from and the test event that verifies it. JIRA carries the design work, and Confluence the rationale. Where an interface carries operator-facing data, like update rate, latency, status fields, or confidence and freshness, the ICD carries the human factors requirement too.

**Evidence, and where it stops.** At Amazon the latency spec shipped as two tiers with pass criteria per interaction type. I haven't written into a defense ICD; the logic is the same.

**The decision it drives.** Every HF finding either becomes a traceable requirement or gets closed with a reason.

## Setting a requirement from human data (latency) — Tier 1 · REVISED

> **Question:** "How do you set a requirement when nobody knows the right number?" Or: *"Tell me about your most impactful project."*

### Answer

**The claim.** Thresholds should come from people, measured with control, and ship as a requirement with its verification method and its limits.

**How I'd know.** I built a Wizard-of-Oz rig that set the response delay on every trial, to the millisecond: thirty participants, twelve interaction types, six delay levels from half a second to three seconds, about two thousand trials. People rated each response on a unipolar three-point slowness scale, because people notice slowness, not speed.

**The requirement.** Two tiers per interaction type, read off the rating distribution rather than the mean. The high tier is the delay where more than seventy percent rate it not slow and fewer than five percent rate it too slow. The acceptable tier is more than half not slow and under fifteen percent too slow. Verification: measure fielded latency against the band, pass or fail.

**Evidence, and where it stops.** Engineering adopted the thresholds as targets, and an economics team's model projected about fifty million dollars. I held workload constant. For pilots, I'd manipulate workload and time-on-task fatigue.

**The decision it drives.** A threshold, a tolerance, and a pass criterion an engineer can test without me in the room.

### Follow-ups

**Follow-up: "Why constant stimuli, not a staircase?"**

> A staircase finds one point. I needed two lines plus the slope, and engineers have to accept the method.

**Follow-up: "A voice assistant isn't an aircraft."**

> Agreed, and the numbers don't carry over. The method does: when a person depends on an automated response, the timing requirement comes from human data, measured under control. Here I'd add the load I held constant.

> **Question:** "How would you run a heuristic analysis of a prototype hardware and software interface?"

### Answer

**The method.** Criteria from MIL-STD-1472H plus task-specific heuristics: mode visibility, alert discriminability, gloved and encumbered input, legibility at viewing distance and at night. Two evaluators work independently, then reconcile.

**Where it stops.** A heuristic finds violations, not performance. The high-severity ones get confirmed in a human-in-the-loop evaluation.

## Span of control and multi-aircraft supervision — Tier 1 · NEW

> **Question:** "How many aircraft can one operator supervise, and how would you find out?"

### Answer

**The claim.** The operator-to-aircraft ratio is a capacity number you measure, not one you choose.

**How I'd know.** Olsen and Goodrich frame it as neglect time, how long an aircraft can run unattended before performance drops, versus interaction time, how long it takes to bring it back. Fan-out is neglect time plus interaction time, divided by interaction time. Real capacity is lower, because of switching costs, waiting, and rebuilding SA. So I'd ramp the number of aircraft in a sim with seeded events and track time to detect, error catch rate, and workload.

**The threshold.** Capacity is the point where verification collapses: past a rate, operators stop checking and start accepting. That's a number I can put in a requirement.

**The decision it drives.** A span-of-control requirement with its conditions. Add automation when interaction time is mostly routine work; add operators when neglect time is short.

**What would change my mind.** If performance holds past the predicted ratio, the bottleneck is somewhere else, and I'd find it before raising the number.

## Human-autonomy transparency — Tier 1 · NEW

> **Question:** "What does the operator need to see about what the autonomy is doing, and why?"

### Answer

**The claim.** What it's doing, why, and what it will do next, with the uncertainty attached.

**How I'd know.** Chen and colleagues' situation-awareness-based agent transparency model gives three levels: current state and plan, the reasoning behind it, and the projection with its uncertainty. The failure to design against is automation surprise, a mode change the operator didn't see. My patent was on context-dependent control mapping, where one input means different things in different modes, which is exactly where mode errors come from.

**Where it stops.** More transparency costs attention, so display density gets tested under load.

**The decision it drives.** Requirements for mode annunciation, intent display, and keeping confidence separate from input freshness, verified with SA probes and response time to surprise events.

## Workload and SA for flight — Tier 1 · NEW

### Answer

## Fatigue across a long mission — Tier 1 · NEW

> **Question:** "How would you measure fatigue across a long mission and turn it into a requirement?"

### Answer

**The claim.** Fatigue is time on task and time of day, so I measure across the full mission duration and turn the decrement into design rules.

**How I'd know.** The psychomotor vigilance task for reaction-time lapses, the Karolinska Sleepiness Scale, and the Samn-Perelli fatigue checklist at fixed intervals, plus performance on embedded tasks. That needs long sim runs, not short sessions.

**Evidence, and where it stops.** My dissertation was on working memory and reaction time under high-stress task interruption. Lab fatigue isn't operational fatigue, so I'd confirm it at test events.

**The decision it drives.** Requirements for alerting sized for the late-mission operator, task scheduling that doesn't stack critical tasks late, and handoff rules for maximum continuous supervision, each verified at full duration.

## Automation trust with skeptical pilots — Tier 1 · REVISED

> **Question:** "Pilots are skeptical of autonomy. How do you design for, and measure, appropriate trust?"

### Answer

**The claim.** I'm after appropriate reliance. Over-trust and disuse are both failures, and in a pilot community disuse may be the bigger risk.

**How I'd know.** Lee and See frame trust as calibration to what the system can actually do. Parasuraman and Manzey show complacency appears when operators juggle several tasks, which is what supervising several aircraft is. So I measure reliance against reliability: over-trust shows up as acceptance going flat across confidence bands, disuse as overrides of correct recommendations.

**Evidence, and where it stops.** At Mercedes I studied takeover alerts in Level 2 and Level 3 handovers. That was drivers, not pilots.

**The decision it drives.** Reliance gets logged, revalidated after every model change, and earned through predictability, with pilots in the test events.

### Say this

- *"Trust is earned by predictability, and measured against reliability."*

## Degraded conditions in flight autonomy — Tier 1 · REVISED

> **Question:** "The link degrades or drops mid-mission. How does the operator know, and how do they recover?"

### Answer

**The claim.** The operator has to know when the system is degraded, not just when it's wrong, and the design has to keep working in that state.

**How I'd know.** I'd build scenarios from field reports and SMEs: lost or degraded link and latency, GPS-denied navigation, sensor degradation, weather and night, autonomy mode changes, fatigue late in a mission, and handoffs between operators or sites. Then I'd measure detection of the degraded state, time to recover, and errors along the way.

**Two rules.** A stale input can produce a high-confidence recommendation, so the display separates confidence from input freshness. And acknowledgment and completion are two signals; collapse them and operators double-actuate. For a lost link, the operator should know in advance what each aircraft will do.

**The decision it drives.** Requirements for degraded-mode indication and defined fallback behavior, plus the scenarios that belong in training.

### Say this

- *"Separate confidence from freshness, and acknowledgment from completion."*

## Standards and models

[[card]] **Standards.** MIL-STD-1472H, human engineering design criteria, September 2020. MIL-HDBK-46855A, the human engineering program process handbook *[VERIFY status and date]*. SAE6906, Standard Practice for HSI, 2019. MIL-STD-1474E, noise limits *[VERIFY date]*. MIL-STD-882E, system safety *[VERIFY whether Change 1 is current]*. DoDI 5000.95, HSI in defense acquisition, 2022. HSI Program Plan data item DI-HFAC-81743 *[VERIFY revision]*.

[[card]] **MIL-STD-882E matrix.** Severity: catastrophic, critical, marginal, negligible. Probability: frequent, probable, occasional, remote, improbable, plus eliminated. Risk: high, serious, medium, low. Mitigation precedence: eliminate by design, reduce by design change, engineered features, warnings, then procedures, training, and PPE.

[[card]] **Fan-out.** Neglect time is how long an asset runs unattended before performance falls below threshold. Interaction time is how long it takes to restore it. Fan-out equals neglect time plus interaction time, divided by interaction time. Switching costs and wait times make real capacity lower.

[[card]] **Agent transparency.** Level one: the agent's current state, goals, and plan. Level two: its reasoning and constraints. Level three: its projection, with uncertainty.

[[card]] **Fatigue scales.** The PVT measures reaction-time lapses. The Karolinska Sleepiness Scale is a nine-point sleepiness rating. Samn-Perelli is a seven-point fatigue rating. The Bedford Workload Scale rates spare capacity.

[[card]] **Levels and stages of automation.** Parasuraman, Sheridan, and Wickens: acquire information, analyze it, select a decision, implement the action, each at its own level. Mapped to a multi-asset mission: plan, task, monitor, intervene or retask.

[[card]] **Signal detection theory.** Hits, misses, false alarms, correct rejections. Sensitivity separates signal from noise, and the criterion sets the threshold. Payoffs and base rate set the right criterion; too many false alarms and operators ignore the alarm.

[[card]] **Multiple resource theory.** Visual and auditory input, spatial and verbal codes, and perception and response draw on separate resources. Two tasks on the same resource interfere more.
